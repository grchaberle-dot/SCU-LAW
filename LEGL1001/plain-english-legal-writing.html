✍️ Plain English in Legal Writing

LEGL1001 | Based on Michael Kirby & Joseph Kimble

Why Plain English?

  • The use of Latin creates unnecessary complications between lawyers and the average folk
  • The law belongs to the people and they have the right to understand it
  • Regular people usually find words with Germanic Anglo-Saxon roots far easier to understand than those with French or Latin backgrounds
  • Plain English is much more clear and brief in comparison
  • It gets rid of unnecessary complications, ambiguities and obscurities that arise from using Latin and French based words

History

  • The Plain English movement came about in the 1970s
  • In the early to mid 1980s, statutes started to be written in 'Plain English'
  • One Judge said such statutes were 'an affront to his sensibilities and that it was an intolerable interference by the ignorant in the mysteries and wholesome traditions of the law'
  • Gradually over time the court became more accepting of Plain English statutes

Source: Michael Kirby, 'How I Learned to Drop Latin and Love Plain Legal Language' (2013) 51(2) Law Society Journal 60.

What Can Lawyers Do To Improve Communication?

  • Choose a single word with Germanic roots rather than many words or those with Latin or French backgrounds
  • Better understand the techniques of communication other than verbal (charts, diagrams, maps, photos, technology)
  • Written communication layout: paragraphing, headings, subheadings and greater use of full stops help the reader follow better

Kirby's Ten Commandments of Plain English

Based on the forty elements of Plain English developed by Professor Joseph Kimble:

1. Begin complex statements of fact and law with a summary (the issue, crucial facts and answer) to let the readers know where they are going
2. Break up long sections and paragraphs. Group related materials together, and order the parts in a sequence that will appear logical to readers
3. Pay careful attention to layout (type size, typeface, white space, indenting, and the like). Use plenty of headings and subheadings to identify the progression of ideas
4. When conveying detailed information, regularly use vertical lists. Where appropriate, number the items
5. Write in short sentences. If a sentence exceeds two or three lines, consider breaking it up
6. Prefer the active voice over the passive
7. Use familiar, concrete words. Avoid jargon. Replace archaic or formal words with modern equivalents
8. Omit unnecessary words. Cut every word that does not add meaning
9. Avoid multiple negatives. State things positively where possible
10. Test the document on a typical reader. Revise, revise, revise

Key Takeaway

The goal: Legal writing should be clear enough that an ordinary person can understand it on first reading. Complexity should come from the ideas, not from the language used to express them.

This is directly relevant to LEGL1001 assessments: your writing will be marked on clarity, structure, and accessibility as much as legal accuracy.
