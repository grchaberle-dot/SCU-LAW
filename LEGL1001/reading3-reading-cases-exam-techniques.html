Determining the Context of the Decision

  • Law students can usually skip over the first step in finding the common law: unearthing the relevant cases. In most courses the casebooks will have already assembled them for you.
  • From your first look, aim to extract a precis of a case: a short summary of the facts and holding, acting out the rule of law established by the precedent
  • The precis will be the building block from which you will construct or neutralise arguments that can be based on that precedent
  • It should contain whatever information is needed to apply or distinguish the holding in future analogous situations

Understanding the Context

  • The initial step: ascertain who is suing whom for what
  • Although this seems mechanical, it is not always straightforward (it requires a little detective work)
  • The cases you read are appellate decisions or questions of law (in some jurisdictions first instance decisions are reported too)
  • The judges giving the decision have heard counsel for the parties explain in great detail the facts giving rise to the dispute, the presentation of hearings, and legal events prior to trial
  • Often details of the original trial itself are included
  • The appellate judges then deliver an opinion on their interpretation of a point of law without necessarily setting out all the background context clearly
  • It is important that you carefully read a decision to draw out this information. You cannot understand the relevance of a judgment unless you understand the factual basis for the case

Navigating Party Names

  • The more times a decision is appealed, the further complicated the trail becomes
  • In some jurisdictions the names of the parties reverse as one party and then the other appeals: Smith v Jones becomes Jones v Smith
  • Sometimes parties are added or dropped at various 'appeals' stages
  • Some parties appear as 'third parties' and sometimes the action is joined by the States of Victoria and Queensland as interveners when the case is appealed if those States see their powers threatened by the original decision
  • Labels attached to the various parties change too: plaintiffs and defendants may become appellants, applicants or respondents
  • Judges will usually refer to parties by their judicial labels (appellant, plaintiff, etc), not their real names. And they will do so inconsistently: one judge might speak of the appellant while another refers to the same party as the plaintiff

Distinguishing Ratio Decidendi and Obiter Dicta in a Case

  • The theoretical key to the common law system of precedent is the all-important distinction between ratio decidendi and obiter dicta in a case
  • The ratio decidendi is the actual rule of law stated in the holding of a decision, a rule that will be binding on future courts
  • An obiter dictum, on the other hand, is a judicial pronouncement on the law that is not integral to the holding itself
  • While it may be considered by a later court, an obiter dictum will not establish a rule of law that must be followed by any court
  • Although this distinction is for the most part merely a legal myth, it is carefully retained by lawyers who utilise it to formulate legal arguments
Recognising this fact, you should read and summarise cases in a manner that will enable you to argue a rule is the ratio decidendi or obiter dictum, as the need arises, when an analogous hypothetical situation appears in an exam question.
  • On some rare occasions, judges will assist you to identify the ratio or obiter by couching part of their decision in hypothetical terms. For example:
"An alternative ground relied on by the appellant was... and if I were to decide on that basis I would probably conclude that... However, since I am basing my decision on the first argument presented by the appellant, I do not have to decide on the basis of this alternative argument."
  • Presumably the entire discussion of the alternative argument and the conclusion to which it would lead are obiter
  • While they could, and most likely would, be cited by a counsel hoping to rely on a similar argument in a future case, opposing counsel should be able to present it as non-binding dicta
  • This does not mean the argument would fail. An obiter dictum in one case may become ratio in a later one

What the Case Doesn't Tell You

  • As you prepare your precis you should note not only the details of the case, but also their immediate relevance to the holding
  • This preparation will provide you with the tools for constructing and dismantling arguments in an exam context
  • The facts that were absent in the original case are as important as those which were present

Worked Example: Parker v The South Eastern Railway Co

  • In Parker, a customer had deposited a bag in a cloak-room at the defendant's railway station, paid the clerk 2d, and received a paper ticket, on one side of which was printed a number and a date and the words "See back"
  • The reverse side contained a number of clauses relating to articles left by passengers, the last of which was "The company will not be responsible for any package exceeding the value of £10"
  • A notice to the same effect was printed and hung up in the cloak room
  • The customer (plaintiff) had left a bag worth £24 in the cloakroom and sued for compensation when the railway lost the bag
  • At trial he claimed he had not seen the notice in the cloak room and had not read the front or back of the ticket. He further claimed he had thought the ticket was simply a receipt for money paid by him

The Ratio of Parker

  • Although Parker is considered one of the leading precedents in the area, at the end of the day we don't know if the customer in that case won or lost
  • The appellate court that established the rule of law sent the case back for retrial to see whether there was reasonable notice in that particular case
  • The results of the retrial were never reported
  • At the retrial, the judge (or jury) might conclude one large sign placed so as to be noticed by anyone in the cloak-room would be sufficient to make the customer aware of the conditions
  • But if the room had two dozen posters in it, of which the notice of conditions was only one, the judge might conclude the company had not done enough
  • The answer to the question is not to be found in Parker

Applying Parker to Exam Problems

  • In any case following Parker, the customer's lawyer will seize upon all the facts peculiar to the subsequent case to show why the basic condition stipulated in Parker (making the customer aware of the existence of the conditions) was not satisfied
  • The process is two-fold:
    1. Show the railway's actions in the subsequent case were insufficient to make customers aware of the conditions
    2. In the alternative: argue that even if the railway's actions were sufficient to make customers aware generally, they were not sufficient with regard to this particular client
  • In this case, she will have to convince the court that the rule in Parker was meant to apply to each individual customer, not the railway's average customer

Exam Application: Transposing Parker

  • It is important to realise that the exam problem is unlikely to be based on a customer depositing a case with a railway
  • Instead it will deal with another contract problem: a customer leaving a jacket at a dry cleaners, a commuter parking a car in a city lot, a child leaving a bicycle in a shop for a new tyre, a customer checking a jacket at the opera
  • The analogies and arguments that arise out of the Parker doctrines must be transposed into completely different factual situations
  • This is a process that requires practice

Using the Precis

  • Reading and summarising cases gives you the knowledge to begin learning the law
  • You will become aware of some of the issues that arise in discussions and recognise arguments that you can use to apply or distinguish the precedents in analogous situations
  • Many more ideas will flow from the lecturer's analysis of the case and classroom discussion
  • Both will also be of use in understanding how the decision and arguments arising out of a case relate to those raised in other cases and other areas of law
  • How to learn best and apply this knowledge in the context of a law course geared towards evaluation by means of a final examination are explored in the next chapter

Donoghue v Stevenson [1932]: The Full Story

House of Lords (1932)

Extract from the Judgment of Lord Atkin:

  • "In this way it can be ascertained at any time whether the law recognises a duty, but only where the case can be referred to some particular species which has been examined and class filed"
  • "And yet the duty which is common to all the cases where liability is established must logically be based upon some element common to the cases where it is found to exist"
  • "To seek a complete logical definition of the general principle is probably to go beyond the function of the judge, for the more general the definition the more likely it is to omit essentials or to introduce non-essentials"
"The rule that you are to love your neighbour becomes in law, you must not injure your neighbour; and the lawyer's question, 'Who is my neighbour?' receives a restricted reply. You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour. Who, then, in law is my neighbour? The answer seems to be: persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so affected when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question."

Lord Atkin, Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC at 580.

Facts and Procedural History

  • The ginger beer was manufactured by the respondent to be sold as a drink. It was in opaque bottles (containing the appellant) that it was bought by the appellant and labelled by it in its with a label bearing his name
  • The bottles were thereafter sealed with a metal cap by the respondent
  • She further averred that it was the duty of the respondent to provide a system of working a business which would not allow snails to get into his ginger beer bottles, and that even able to his duty to provide an efficient system of inspection of the bottles before the ginger beer was filled into them, and that he failed in both these duties and had so caused the mischief
  • The respondent objected that these averments were irrelevant and insufficient to support the conclusions of the summons
  • The Lord Ordinary held that the averments disclosed a good cause of action and allowed a proof
  • The Second Division by a majority (the Lord Justice Clerk, Lord Ormidale and Lord Anderson; Lord Hunter dissenting) recalled the interlocutor of the Lord Ordinary and dismissed the action

Key Takeaways

  1. Context first: always determine who is suing whom for what before anything else
  2. Party names change on appeal: track them carefully, use a chart if needed
  3. Ratio vs obiter in practice: judges sometimes signal obiter with hypothetical language ("if I were to decide on that basis...")
  4. What's absent matters: missing facts are as important as present facts for exam arguments
  5. Precis = building block: your case summary should contain enough to apply OR distinguish the holding
  6. Transpose, don't replicate: exam problems will use different facts but the same legal principles. Practice moving the doctrine to new scenarios.
  7. The neighbour principle (Lord Atkin): take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour
