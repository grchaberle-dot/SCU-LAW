Court Hierarchies

Federal Hierarchy

  • High Court of Australia (Full Court / Single Judge) sits at the top
  • Below: Family Court (Full Court), Federal Court (Full Court), State/Territory Supreme Court (Full Court or Court of Appeal)
  • Below those (single judge level): Family Court, Federal Court, State/Territory Supreme Court
  • Below: Federal Circuit Court and State/Territory District/County Court
  • Bottom: State/Territory Local/Magistrates Court
  • Appeals only where allowed by statute, regulation or common law rule

State Courts and Tribunals

  • Each state court system has a superior court called the Supreme Court
  • Supreme Courts have appellate and some original jurisdiction, both civil and criminal, and deal with state and also federal matters
  • Below the Supreme Court there are usually two levels of courts: an intermediate court and lower courts
  • In the states with an intermediate court (all except Tasmania), that court has similar jurisdiction to the Supreme Court but with some financial or other limits in civil matters, and a limit on the types of offence it may try in its criminal jurisdiction
  • Most jurisdictions also have a wide jurisdiction civil and administrative appeals tribunal
  • In some jurisdictions there are several individual specialist tribunals dealing with matters such as industrial relations and land and the environment
  • With the special leave of the High Court an appeal may lie from a decision of the appellate level state Supreme Court, on state matters, to the High Court

Queensland

  • Supreme Court of Queensland: both appellate and original jurisdiction in both civil and criminal matters. The Trial Division, constituted by a single judge, may exercise either original or appellate jurisdiction, while the Court of Appeal hears only appeals
  • District Courts of Queensland: extensive civil and criminal jurisdiction at first instance and, in certain circumstances, on appeal from the Magistrates' Courts
  • Magistrates Courts with limited civil and criminal jurisdiction
  • There is a Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal with general administrative jurisdiction

South Australia

  • Supreme Court of South Australia: original and general appellate jurisdiction in civil and criminal matters. A single judge may exercise either original or appellate jurisdiction while the Full Court exercises appellate jurisdiction only
  • District Court of South Australia: extensive civil and criminal jurisdiction. It has a Civil Division, a Criminal Division, and a Criminal Injuries Division
  • Magistrates Court with limited civil and criminal jurisdiction
  • Appeals from Magistrates Courts may be taken to the District Court or directly to the Supreme Court

New South Wales

  • An appeal on a question of law from the Civil and Administrative Tribunal of NSW may be made directly to the Supreme Court
  • Appeals from the Local Courts can be made to the District Court, or directly to the NSW Supreme Court or, where appropriate, to the Federal Court
  • There is a limited right of appeal from the District Court to the Court of Appeal or to the Court of Criminal Appeal

Canadian Courts and Tribunals

  • The main courts in the Canadian federal system, with judges federally appointed
  • Supreme Court of Canada: highest court in the Canadian hierarchy with both civil and criminal appellate jurisdiction. It hears appeals from the Federal Court of Appeal and from provincial Courts of Appeal. It also has the authority to deliver advisory opinions.
  • Federal Court of Appeal: hears appeals from the Federal Court (Trial Division), the Tax Court of Canada, and also adjudicates appeals under various federal Acts

United States of America

  • Unlike the four court systems described above, the American system is not a unified one
  • There are two court systems, one at the state level and the other at the federal level
  • The state courts concern themselves with matters falling within state jurisdiction under the American Constitution, while the federal courts concern themselves with matters falling within federal jurisdiction under the Constitution
  • All states have a superior court. While they are usually referred to as supreme courts, some (such as in Maryland) are known as courts of appeal
  • With respect to the federal court system, the highest court of appeal is the Supreme Court of the United States
  • With rare exceptions, appeals cannot be taken from a decision of a state supreme court or court of appeal to a federal court
  • The exception relates to the raising of a constitutional matter in the course of proceedings in the state courts

Case Law and Precedent

Exercise 7: Analysing a Case

If a detailed analysis is required, it may be necessary to make a note of a wider range of matters, including some or all of the following:

  1. Citation
  2. Court, including single judge, Full Court or Court of Appeal
  3. Brief statement of material facts
  4. Procedural history
  5. Grounds for appeal and/or issues to be decided
  6. Summary of court's analysis of law
  7. Principle of law to be applied
  8. Description of how law was applied to the facts
  9. Decision
  10. Orders made by the court
  11. Any features of the case suggesting that it should be viewed in its social or cultural context

Steps (f)–(i) will need to be repeated for each issue that the court discusses. And where the decision includes more than one judgment, those steps will need to be repeated for those reasons on which there is a divergence of opinion. Matters (g), (h), (i) and (j) correspond with the steps in the IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) discussed further in Chapters 18, 19 and 21.

Case Report Structure

  • A report of a case and a detailed analysis follow. The case has been edited slightly.
  • Citation: appears in a box at the top
  • Decision: the orders the court made, usually at the very end of a judgment. They record the legal consequences ordered by the court following on from its reasons
  • Orders made by the court: eg "Summons dismissed with the plaintiff to pay costs"
  • Social or cultural context: not all judgments will contain material of note in this step. This case, being relatively recent, does not contain any notable features in this regard

Worked Case: Hart v Rankin [1977] WAR 144

Citation

Hart v Rankin [1977] WAR 144
Supreme Court of Western Australia
Burt CJ
15, 30 September 1977

Catchwords

Criminal law and procedure — Driving offence — Motor vehicle — Driver — Whether person in charge of motor car under tow driving a motor vehicle — Road Traffic Act 1974–1978 ss 4(01), 5(1).

Facts

  • The appellant was charged upon each of three complaints that he had driven a motor vehicle on a road while not holding a driver's licence contrary to s 49(2) of the Road Traffic Act 1974–1978
  • He was convicted of each charge upon his pleas of guilty
  • He had made three journeys in his car between 4.30 in the afternoon and 5.30 the following morning
  • On the last occasion he sat in the driver's seat of his car and steered it while under tow
  • He appealed against his conviction in respect of the last offence, contending that his car was not a motor vehicle within the meaning of the Act and that he was not driving it

Held

  1. Upon the proper construction of s 4(01) of the Road Traffic Act 1974–1978, the appellant drove a motor vehicle at the material time because:
    • (a) the car was a motor vehicle as defined by s 5(1) and remained a motor vehicle notwithstanding that at the material time it was under tow and was unable to propel itself (it had not ceased to be a motor vehicle)
    • (b) while the verb 'to drive' was not defined by the Road Traffic Act, the appellant was the driver of the motor vehicle as defined by s 5(1) of the Act since he was in fact in control of the motor vehicle and, as such, was its driver

R v MacDonagh (1974) 2 WLR 529 at 531; (1974) 2 All ER 257 at 259, applied.
Wallace v Major [1946] 2 All ER 87; Caughey v Spacek (1966) VR 600 and McGrath v Cooper (1976) VR 315, distinguished.

Worked Case: Warringah Properties v Babic [2006] NSWSC 702

Citation

Warringah Properties Pty Ltd v Babic [2006] NSWSC 702
Supreme Court of New South Wales
Malpass AsJ
10, 14 July 2006

Key Issue

Claim for restoration of dividing fence — destroyed by deliberate act — distinction between fence and retaining wall — fence may have other functions — legislative changes — intention of the legislature — indemnity costs and leave.

Facts

  • The Plaintiff and the Defendants are respectively the owners of adjoining properties
  • It is now common ground that what has been described as a sandstone structure (the structure) stood at least in a rough sense upon the common boundary between the two properties
  • The dispute that has ensued between the parties came into being when the Plaintiff demolished the structure on or about 10 May 2005
  • The active participant on the part of the Plaintiff was the late Mr Humphreys (a director and licensed builder)

Legislation Considered

  • Dividing Fences Act 1991 (NSW) (the 1991 Act): sections 3, 7 and 13
  • The Act introduced provisions which were different to those previously found in the Dividing Fences Act 1951 (the 1951 Act)
  • Section 3 of the Act contains definitions of 'dividing fence' and 'fence'
  • The definition of 'fence' excludes 'inter alia a retaining wall'

Key Definitions

'dividing fence' means a fence separating the land of adjoining owners, whether at the common boundary of adjoining lands or on a line other than the common boundary.

'fence' means a structure, ditch or embankment, or a hedge or similar vegetable barrier, enclosing or bounding land, whether or not continuous or extending along the whole of the boundary separating the land of the adjoining owners, and includes:
(a) any gate, cattlegrid or apparatus necessary for the maintenance of the fence; and
(b) any natural or artificial watercourse which separates the land of adjoining owners; and
(c) any foundation or support necessary for the support and maintenance of the fence,
but does not include a retaining wall or a wall which is part of a house, garage or other building.

Analysis

  • The definition of 'fence' can be seen to be one of great width
  • Save for the exclusions, it would appear to be contemplated to pick up any structure that has the characteristics of enclosing or bounding land
  • A fence satisfies the statutory requirements of a 'dividing fence' if it separates the land of adjoining owners
  • The concept of separation has been said to have a functional connotation
  • Court concluded that when Dwayne's head hit the step it had the character of a fence under the 1991 Act even though it was retaining wall as well

Key Takeaways

  1. Federal hierarchy: High Court > Federal/Family/State Supreme (Full Court) > Single judge > District/County > Magistrates
  2. State hierarchies vary but follow the same general pattern (Supreme > Intermediate > Lower)
  3. Case analysis checklist: citation, court, facts, procedural history, grounds, law analysis, principle, application, decision, orders, context
  4. IRAC maps onto steps (g)–(j) of the case analysis framework
  5. Statutory interpretation in action: the Warringah Properties case shows how courts work through statutory definitions element by element
  6. Hart v Rankin demonstrates how courts interpret undefined terms ('to drive') by looking at purpose and context, not just dictionary meaning
