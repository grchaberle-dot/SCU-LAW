Chapter: 4 – Legal Positivism

Key names: Jeremy Bentham, John Austin, Hans Kelsen, H L A Hart, Joseph Raz

What Is Legal Positivism?

Legal positivism denies any necessary connection between law and morality. What counts as law is fixed by social facts — who enacted it, whether officials accept it — not by whether it is just.

The sources thesis

The validity of a legal rule depends on its source, not its merit. To find out whether something is law, you look at where it came from, not whether it is good.

The separation thesis

Law and morality are conceptually distinct. "This is law" and "this is just" are two separate questions, and answering one does not answer the other.

Exam trap: the separation thesis does not claim law and morality never overlap in content, and it does not claim you should obey unjust law. Students lose marks constantly on this.

Exclusive and inclusive positivism

VersionClaimChampion
Exclusive (hard)Moral criteria can never determine legal validity. Sources only.Raz
Inclusive (soft)Moral criteria may determine validity, but only if a legal system's own rule of recognition incorporates them (e.g. a bill of rights)Later Hart

Descriptive and normative positivism

  • Descriptive positivism – a claim about what law is. Hart's core project.
  • Normative positivism – a claim that we should keep law and morality conceptually separate, because doing so preserves our ability to criticise the law rather than sanctifying it. Bentham's motivation.

John Austin (1790–1859)

The command theory

Austin's answer to "what is law?": law is the command of the sovereign, backed by a sanction.

Three components:

  • Command – an expression of a wish that another act or forbear, plus the power and intention to inflict harm if they do not.
  • Sovereign – the person or body who is habitually obeyed by the bulk of the population, and who habitually obeys no one else.
  • Sanction – the threatened evil that gives the command its force.

Implications Austin accepted

The theory is admirably consistent, and its consequences are why it eventually collapsed:

  • Customary and Indigenous law are not "real" law — no identifiable sovereign issuing commands.
  • International law is not law — it is merely "positive morality", because no sovereign stands above states.
  • Constitutional law is not binding law — the sovereign cannot command itself, so constitutional limits are political facts rather than legal rules.
Use this: Austin's implications are the cleanest way to show why Hart had to build something better. Do not just say Austin was wrong; show what his theory forced him to deny.

Hans Kelsen (1881–1973)

The pure theory of law

Kelsen wanted a theory of law purified of everything that is not law: no morality, no politics, no sociology, no psychology. Just the legal.

Norms

A norm is a statement that something ought to be. Law is a system of norms, and Kelsen insists that norms cannot be reduced to facts — the ought is irreducible.

Subjective and objective meaning

A gunman demanding your wallet and a tax officer demanding payment are behaviourally similar: both express a wish backed by threat. Both have the same subjective meaning. The difference is that the tax demand also has an objective meaning as a valid norm, because it is authorised by a higher norm. The gunman's demand is not.

Law as a coercive order

Kelsen sees legal norms as fundamentally directed at officials: they instruct officials to apply a sanction when certain conditions are met. "If a person steals, an official shall impose punishment."

The basic norm (Grundnorm)

Every norm gets its validity from a higher norm. A council by-law is valid because a statute authorises it; the statute is valid because the constitution authorises it. But the chain cannot regress forever.

Kelsen terminates it with the basic norm: a presupposed norm directing that the historically first constitution ought to be obeyed. It is not enacted by anyone. Kelsen calls it a transcendental-logical presupposition — something we must assume in order to interpret the legal order as a system of valid norms at all.

Unity of the legal system

Because every norm traces back to the same basic norm, all the norms of a legal system form a single unified hierarchy. Kelsen extended this to argue for the unity of domestic and international law (monism).

Kelsen on law and morality — and his late reversal

For most of his career Kelsen held that the basic norm is binding and blinding: it makes the legal order binding while blinding us to questions of moral merit.

Important: in his final work, General Theory of Norms, Kelsen reversed himself and concluded the basic norm is a fiction rather than a presupposition — a useful invention, not something logically required. Mentioning this late reversal is an easy way to show depth of reading.

H L A Hart (1907–1992)

Key text: The Concept of Law (OUP, 1961; 3rd ed 2012). The most important work of 20th-century Anglo-American jurisprudence.

Criticisms of Austin

  • Not all laws are commands backed by sanctions. Contract law, marriage law and will-making laws do not order you to do anything — they give you powers to create legal relations. Calling a failed will "sanctioned" is a distortion.
  • Habitual obedience cannot explain continuity. When a sovereign dies, why is the successor obeyed from day one, before any habit has formed? Only a rule about succession explains it.
  • Persistence of laws. Statutes enacted centuries ago bind us today, though no living person habitually obeys their author.
  • The gunman writ large. Austin's theory cannot distinguish law from the demands of an armed robber.

Being obliged vs having an obligation

Hart's key move. If a gunman demands your money, you are obliged to hand it over — a statement about your situation and your fear. But you have no obligation to do so.

Contrast a red light with no one around: you are not obliged to stop (no threat, no consequence), but you do have an obligation to stop. Austin's theory can only capture the first. Hart's aim is to explain the second.

The practice theory of rules

A social rule exists when there is (a) a regular pattern of behaviour, and (b) a critical reflective attitude towards that pattern — deviation is treated as a fault and criticism is regarded as legitimate.

Internal and external points of view

ViewpointDescription
ExternalThe observer who records only regularities of behaviour. Hart's Martian: sees that cars stop at red lights, can predict it, but has no concept of a reason to stop.
InternalThe participant who accepts the rule as a standard of conduct and uses it to guide and criticise behaviour, including their own.
HermeneuticThe theorist's position: understanding and describing the internal point of view without personally endorsing it. Illustrated by the David and Alison vegetarian dinner example — you can fully grasp why someone treats a rule as binding without sharing their commitment.

Primary and secondary rules

  • Primary rules – impose duties. Do not steal; do not assault.
  • Secondary rules – are about primary rules. They confer powers to create, change, apply and identify them.

The three defects and their cures

Imagine a society with primary rules only. It suffers three defects, and each secondary rule is the remedy:

DefectProblemCure
UncertaintyNo way to settle what the rules areRule of recognition
Static characterRules change only by slow driftRules of change
InefficiencyNo authoritative way to settle breachesRules of adjudication

Hart calls the union of primary and secondary rules "the heart of a legal system". This table is the most examinable content in the chapter.

The rule of recognition

The master test for legal validity in a system. It is not itself valid or invalid — it simply exists as a social practice, constituted by the convergent behaviour and internal attitude of officials.

Compare Kelsen: the Grundnorm is a presupposition; the rule of recognition is a social fact. Kelsen's is logical, Hart's is sociological. That contrast is a ready-made exam paragraph.

The minimum content of natural law

Hart's concession to natural law. Given certain contingent truths about human nature, any viable legal system must contain certain rules. The five truisms:

  • Human vulnerability – we can be harmed, so rules restricting violence are needed.
  • Approximate equality – no one is strong enough to dominate all others permanently, so cooperation is necessary.
  • Limited altruism – we are neither angels nor devils, so rules are both necessary and possible.
  • Limited resources – scarcity requires rules of property and exchange.
  • Limited understanding and strength of will – people will not always comply voluntarily, so sanctions are needed.

Crucially this is a claim about survival, not about justice. A legal system could satisfy all five and still be monstrous. Hart remains a positivist.

Joseph Raz (1939–2022)

Hart's student, and the leading exclusive positivist.

The service conception of authority

An authority is legitimate when following its directives helps you comply better with the reasons that already apply to you than you would by deciding for yourself. Authority serves the subject.

Exclusionary reasons

An authoritative directive is not just another reason to be weighed against your other reasons. It is a second-order reason that excludes some first-order reasons from the balance.

Raz's supervisor example: if your supervisor tells you to do something, you do not add "my supervisor said so" to your list of pros and cons. The instruction replaces the deliberation.

Law claims authority

Raz's central argument for the sources thesis: law necessarily claims legitimate authority. It may not actually possess it, but it must claim it.

For a directive to serve that function, you must be able to identify what it requires without reasoning about the underlying moral merits — otherwise it could not replace your deliberation. Therefore legal validity must depend on sources alone. This is the argument for exclusive positivism.

Three features of legal systems

  • Comprehensive – claims authority to regulate any type of behaviour.
  • Supreme – claims authority to regulate the establishment and operation of all other institutions within its territory.
  • Open – adopts and enforces norms that do not originate within it (contracts, foreign law, custom).

No generic obligation to obey

Raz denies there is any general moral obligation to obey the law simply because it is the law. Whether you should comply depends on the content of the particular law and your circumstances. A striking position for a positivist to hold, and a good essay hook.
