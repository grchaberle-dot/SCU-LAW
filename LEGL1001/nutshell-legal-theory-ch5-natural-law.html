Overview

This chapter covers the modern revival of natural law theory, focusing on two major figures: Lon Fuller (procedural natural law) and John Finnis (substantive natural law in the Thomistic tradition). It then examines challenges to Finnis and introduces Murphy as the leading post-Finnis natural law scholar.

The chapter builds directly on Chapter 3 (classical natural law: Augustine, Aquinas) and Chapter 4 (legal positivism: Austin, Hart). You need both of those to make sense of the arguments here.

Part 1: Lon Fuller — The Internal Morality of Law

The core idea

Fuller argues that law has an inner morality: a set of procedural requirements that any system must satisfy to count as a legal system at all. This is not about what the law says (its content) but about how it operates (its form).

The eight principles (the "inner morality")

A legal system must meet these requirements to function as law:

  • Generality — laws must be general rules, not ad hoc commands
  • Promulgation — laws must be published and accessible
  • Non-retroactivity — laws should not apply backwards in time
  • Clarity — laws must be understandable
  • Non-contradiction — laws must not contradict each other
  • Possibility — laws must not demand the impossible
  • Constancy — laws should not change so frequently that people cannot orient their conduct
  • Congruence — official action must match the declared rules

Why this matters

Fuller's claim is that a system that completely fails on any of these eight dimensions is not merely a bad legal system — it is not a legal system at all. The eight principles are not optional extras; they are constitutive of legality itself.

This is a natural law argument because it ties the concept of law to certain standards — but it's a procedural rather than substantive natural law theory. Fuller doesn't say the content of law must be just; he says the form of law must meet these conditions.

Hart's criticisms of Fuller

  • Morality of purpose vs morality of duty. Fuller's eight principles are more like conditions for efficacy (making law work) than for morality (making law good). A poisoner needs to follow procedures to poison effectively; that doesn't make poisoning moral.
  • The Nazi regime example. Nazi Germany could satisfy Fuller's procedural criteria while being profoundly unjust. Procedural morality doesn't guarantee substantive morality.
  • The analogy problem. Calling these principles a "morality" stretches the term. They're better understood as principles of legal craftsmanship or efficacy.

Fuller's response

The eight principles are genuinely moral because they respect human agency. A system that promulgates clear, prospective, general rules treats citizens as rational agents capable of planning their lives. A system that rules by secret, retroactive, contradictory decrees treats citizens as objects to be manipulated. The connection to morality is real, not metaphorical.

Part 2: John Finnis — Natural Law and Natural Rights

Who is Finnis?

John Finnis is the most influential natural law theorist of the late 20th century. His 1980 book Natural Law and Natural Rights revived the Thomistic tradition in a form engaging directly with modern analytical philosophy and Hart's legal positivism.

The seven basic goods

Finnis identifies seven forms of human good that are self-evidently valuable:

  • Life — physical existence, health, vitality
  • Knowledge — understanding for its own sake (not merely instrumental)
  • Play — engaging in activities for their own sake, without ulterior purpose
  • Aesthetic experience — appreciation of beauty in art and nature
  • Sociability (friendship) — harmony between people, from acquaintance to deep friendship
  • Practical reasonableness — the capacity to order one's life through free choices
  • Religion (or spirituality) — concern with questions about meaning and our place in the cosmos

These are self-evident: they cannot be derived from anything more basic. If asked "why is knowledge good?", the only honest answer is that it is good in itself.

Incommensurability

The basic goods are incommensurable. There is no common measure by which you can rank them. You cannot say knowledge is more valuable than friendship in any objective way. Different people can reasonably prioritise differently.

Practical reasonableness

Practical reasonableness is both one of the seven goods and the capacity by which we order our pursuit of all the others. Finnis derives nine requirements:

  • A coherent plan of life
  • No arbitrary preference among basic goods
  • No arbitrary preference among persons
  • Detachment from specific projects (not treating any one commitment as absolute)
  • Commitment to projects once undertaken
  • Limited relevance of consequences
  • Respect for every basic value in every act (never directly act against a basic good)
  • The common good (concern for the community)
  • Following one's conscience

The critique of utilitarianism

Utilitarianism fails because it presupposes what cannot exist: a single scale on which all goods can be measured. If the basic goods are incommensurable, the project of maximising "total good" is incoherent — there is nothing to maximise because there is no common unit of measurement. This is not a practical objection; it is an objection to the logical possibility of the exercise.

Part 3: Finnis on Community and Subsidiarity

Friendship and community

A true community is like a true friendship: concerned with the well-being of its members, not with itself. It is a mutually supportive association for pursuing the common good.

The principle of subsidiarity

The interests of the community are subsidiary to the interests of the individuals who comprise it. A community does not exist for its own sake. Its proper function is to assist individuals in pursuing the basic values. The community exists to help people coordinate their activities for the objectives they have chosen.

Part 4: Finnis's Definition of Law

The focal meaning approach

Finnis contrasts two methods of defining law:

  • Lowest common denominator — find what all legal systems share. Produces a uselessly broad definition.
  • Central case (focal meaning) — identify the clearest example of law that nobody could dispute. Actual systems resemble the central case to greater or lesser degrees.

Finnis selects the practical point of law as the viewpoint: what legal institutions are for.

Finnis's definition

The central case of law (Natural Law and Natural Rights, Ch 10, pp 276–277):

  • A set of rules
  • Made by a determinate and effective authority
  • In accordance with the rule of recognition (Hart's concept)
  • Backed up by sanctions
  • Directed to pursuing the common good of the community

The first four are positivist. The fifth is distinctively natural law. A law with all elements = law in the focal sense. A law with only the positivist criteria = law in a weak or partial sense.

Part 5: Unjust Laws

Finnis does NOT claim an unjust law is "no law at all." An unjust law is not law in the focal sense, but may still be law in a weak sense if it satisfies the rule of recognition.

Four senses of "obligation to obey"

  • Austin's sense (sanction) — you'll be punished. Applies regardless of justice.
  • Hart's legal sense — technical legal obligation via rule of recognition.
  • Moral obligation from common good — an unjust law does NOT generate this.
  • Moral obligation to preserve the system — may exist even for unjust laws, to avoid damaging the legal system as a whole (Aquinas).

Finnis: senses 1 and 2 apply. Sense 3 does not. Sense 4 depends on circumstances.

Part 6: Challenges to Finnis

Ethical status of animals

Finnis: animals lack capacity to reflect on and rationally pursue basic goods, so they have no rights.

Objection: The list was framed for humans. Why not ask what's inherently good for animals? Basic goods operate at both intuitive and reflective levels — why only count the reflective?

The "marital good"

Finnis: marital intercourse between a man and woman = a special basic good, unavailable to same-sex couples.

Objection: On his own theory, same-sex couples can respect all the basic goods. The "marital good" appears motivated by emphasis on procreation, which could be a dimension of the good of life rather than a standalone good.

The global common good

If conceived globally, domestic laws that unreasonably elevate domestic interests at the expense of global justice lose moral force. Finnis is sympathetic but unexplored.

Legal authority

Finnis distinguishes: (1) laws from logical entailment (e.g., murder prohibition), and (2) coordination laws (e.g., which side of the road). Social coordination requires authority because unanimity is impractical.

Objection: Many complex coordination problems ARE solved by convention (languages). Finnis may be overconfident about the necessity of authority.

The strong natural law thesis

Finnis rejects "an unjust law is no law at all" as "pure nonsense" and "self-contradictory." He prefers the weak thesis.

Objection: Ordinary usage often diverges from technical meaning. "Murder" in conversation is broader than in statute. Whether Finnis dismissed the strong thesis too hastily is debatable.

Part 7: Mark C Murphy

Murphy (b. 1968) is arguably the leading defender of natural law theory since Finnis, developing the tradition in new directions.
