Chapter: 5 – Contemporary Natural Law Theories

Key names: J L Mackie, Lon L Fuller, H L A Hart, John Finnis, Mark C Murphy

Read Chapters 3 and 4 first. This chapter is a direct continuation of classical natural law (Ch 3) and an argument against legal positivism (Ch 4). It will not make sense on its own.

Two Challenges for Natural Law Theories

[5.10] Natural law theorists claim there are objective moral values. Two challenges stand in the way, and the whole chapter is structured around answering them:

  • The nature of morality – how can a belief in objective morality be squared with the sheer extent of moral disagreement, and with the fact that values look nothing like the physical properties science deals in?
  • The concept of law – people plainly use the word "law" in a morally neutral sense. We happily call Nazi enactments "laws". How can law be necessarily connected to morality?

The nature of morality: Mackie's two arguments

[5.20] Moral realism holds that moral values exist objectively. Moral nihilism (or error theory) denies this. J L Mackie presses two famous objections:

  • The argument from disagreement – moral views vary radically across cultures and eras. The best explanation is that morality reflects social conditioning, not objective fact.
  • The argument from queerness – if objective values existed, they would be metaphysically very strange entities, unlike anything else in the universe, and we would need a special faculty to perceive them. Better to conclude they do not exist.

The concept of law: Hart's challenge

[5.30] Hart's objection is about ordinary usage. Everyone, including the people who suffered under it, describes Nazi legislation as law — morally repugnant law, but law. If the natural law theorist says these were never laws, they are simply describing something other than what the rest of us mean by the word.

Part 1: Lon L Fuller (1902–1978)

[5.40] Fuller taught at Harvard. His major work is The Morality of Law (1964), and his long-running debate with Hart is one of the set pieces of modern jurisprudence.

The purpose of law

[5.50] Fuller starts from function. The purpose of law is to facilitate human interaction and social harmony by subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules.

Law as a functional concept

[5.60] Some concepts are defined by their purpose rather than their form. A car is a thing for transporting people; something that looks exactly like a car but cannot move is not really a car. Fuller argues law works the same way. If a purported legal system cannot actually guide conduct, it fails to be law regardless of appearances.

The story of King Rex

[5.70] Fuller's famous fable. King Rex sets out to reform his kingdom's law and fails eight separate times — each failure illustrating one requirement of legality. He decides every case ad hoc with no general rules; he makes rules but keeps them secret; he applies rules retroactively; his rules are unintelligible; they contradict each other; they demand the impossible; he changes them daily; and finally his officials administer them inconsistently with what they say.

The moral: Rex did not make bad law. He failed to make law at all.

The eight ways to fail to make law

[5.80] Stated as failures:

  • Failing to make rules at all (deciding everything case by case)
  • Failing to publicise the rules
  • Abusing retroactive legislation
  • Failing to make rules understandable
  • Enacting contradictory rules
  • Requiring conduct beyond the powers of the affected party
  • Changing rules so frequently the subject cannot orient their action
  • Failing to make administration of the rules match their declared content

The internal morality of law

[5.90] Stated positively, the same eight become the internal morality of law:

RequirementWhat it demands
GeneralityLaw must consist of general rules
PromulgationRules must be published and knowable
ProspectivityRules must operate forwards, not backwards
ClarityRules must be understandable
ConsistencyRules must not contradict one another
ObservabilityRules must not demand the impossible
ConstancyRules must be stable enough to plan around
CongruenceOfficial action must match the declared rules

Internal and external moralities

[5.100] The internal morality concerns the form of law — the eight requirements. The external morality concerns the substance: whether the law's content is just. Fuller's theory is about the internal only.

Moralities of duty and aspiration

[5.110] A crucial and frequently missed distinction.

  • Morality of duty – the minimum required. Below this floor, the system's enactments fail to be laws in the first place.
  • Morality of aspiration – above the floor there are degrees. All legal systems should strive to improve their respect for the internal morality.

So a system that meets the duty minimum but falls short on aspiration still produces valid law — it is just a worse legal system than one that does better. This is how Fuller avoids the absurd conclusion that only perfect systems have law.

Hart's criticisms

[5.120] Hart's central objection: Fuller confuses morality with efficacy. The eight requirements are not moral at all; they are conditions for law working effectively, and they are "compatible with very great iniquity" (The Concept of Law, OUP, 3rd ed, 2012, p 207; "Book Review: The Morality of Law" (1965) 78 Harvard Law Review 1281, 1288).

The disagreement crystallises over the Nazi regime:

  • Fuller – Nazi laws failed the duty component of the internal morality. The regime routinely ignored the eight requirements for its own convenience: secret and retrospective enactments to crush opponents, and wide discretion handed to officials.
  • Hart – many Nazi laws were morally repugnant but were still laws, provided they could be recognised as such under the rule of recognition. The moral status of a law should be kept distinct from Fuller's eight "principles of legality", as Hart prefers to call them (1965) 78 Harvard Law Review 1281, 1284.

Fuller's response

Fuller concedes it is theoretically possible for deeply unjust laws to satisfy all eight requirements. But he argues that in practice oppression and legality are unlikely to travel together, so the internal morality operates as a significant safeguard against unjust laws and legal systems.

On the efficacy charge: there is genuine moral value in observing the internal morality, because it advances the purpose of law in facilitating human interaction and social harmony. The internal morality is necessary for human life to be governed by rules, which is in turn necessary for social cooperation. It therefore holds value as a way of promoting human fulfilment.

Fuller and natural law theory

[5.130] Does Fuller belong in the natural law tradition?

Yes: he emphasises the moral purpose of law and argues a law that does not fulfil that purpose is not law in the true sense — a hallmark of classical natural law (Ch 3). Law and morality are necessarily intertwined at the conceptual level because the concept of law contains its own internal morality.

But: his internal morality is not the substantive morality most natural law theorists care about. It covers only the basic procedural requirements needed for law to guide action effectively. Broader notions of justice and human welfare fall within the external morality, which is irrelevant to legal validity.

Fuller is therefore described as a procedural natural law theorist, as distinct from substantive versions.

Further reading: Lon L Fuller, The Morality of Law (Yale University Press, rev ed, 1969) pp 33–44 and 95–107; H L A Hart, "Book Review: The Morality of Law" (1965) 78 Harvard Law Review 1281; Jonathan Crowe, "Between Morality and Efficacy: Reclaiming the Natural Law Theory of Lon Fuller" (2014) 5 Jurisprudence 109.

Part 2: John Finnis (1940– )

[5.140] The most influential contemporary natural law theorist. Emeritus Professor of Law at Oxford and Notre Dame. Completed a law degree at the University of Adelaide, then studied at Oxford as the Rhodes Scholar for South Australia. Major work: Natural Law and Natural Rights (1980). Heavily influenced by Aquinas (Ch 3).

Self-evident values

[5.150] Finnis's answer to both challenges from [5.10] runs through self-evident values. There are basic values self-evident to anyone genuinely engaging in practical reasoning. He calls them "pre-moral": we are aware of them before any substantive moral deliberation.

Self-evident means the values are both indemonstrable and underived — so fundamental and obvious that they both cannot and need not be demonstrated. They cannot reasonably be denied when engaging in practical reflection.

The is–ought gap

[5.160] Natural law theorists are often accused of deriving values from facts — the fallacy Hume identified (A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part I; see Ch 2).

Finnis's response: the basic values are not derived from propositions of fact. Being self-evident, they are not derived from anything. They are simply presupposed by every normative argument. So the leading natural law theorists never committed the error in the first place.

He adds a careful point: cross-cultural agreement that something is valuable is evidence that it is a basic value, but the value is not valuable because people believe it is — that really would be deriving an ought from an is. Rather, people believe these things are valuable because they are valuable.

The seven basic values

[5.170] Together these represent the "basic forms of human flourishing" and encapsulate "everything one could reasonably want to do, to have and to be" (Natural Law and Natural Rights, OUP, 2nd ed, 2011, pp 23, 97).

ValueContent
LifeWorthwhile to preserve our own lives and to protect and nurture the lives of others
KnowledgeWorthwhile to seek knowledge for its own sake; worth knowing which view is true and which false
PlayValue in recreation. All cultures value some form of it, though the forms differ widely
Aesthetic experienceWorthwhile to pursue and value beauty — a sunset, a performance, an artwork, a piece of music
FriendshipWorthwhile to have friends and to co-operate with others in the community
SpiritualityWorthwhile to wonder about the broader questions of human existence — meaning, the origins of the universe
Practical reasonablenessWorthwhile to seek to act reasonably. Has nine components, below

Finnis does not claim the list is definitive — "there is no magic in the number seven" — but thinks it captures all the basic purposes of human action (p 92). All seven are equally fundamental. A person may emphasise particular values in her own life, but we should never deliberately harm or reject any of them.

The value of knowledge

[5.180] Finnis devotes a whole chapter to knowledge, arguing not only that its value is self-evident but that any attempt to deny it is self-refuting. If you deny knowledge is valuable, you implicitly acknowledge it is worthwhile to know whether knowledge is valuable. Engaging in rational discussion at all affirms that knowledge is worth pursuing.

An important limit: this does not prevent denying the value of knowledge of a particular type. "It is not valuable to have knowledge about the physical properties of Mars" is not self-refuting. But "it is not valuable to have practical knowledge about how to act" is self-refuting — in denying it you are actively seeking practical knowledge.

Incommensurability

[5.190] The basic values are incommensurable: none can be reduced to any of the others, and there is no overarching standard by which they can be compared using a single metric. As Finnis puts it, "the basic human goods are all equally and irreducibly basic; none of them is subordinated as a mere means to any of the others" (Fundamentals of Ethics, Georgetown University Press, 1983, p 89).

There is also no hierarchy: "each [basic value] is fundamental. None is more fundamental than any of the others, for each can reasonably be focused upon, and each, when focused upon, claims a priority" (NLNR, 2nd ed, 2011, p 93).

The practical problem. Finnis's own example: you are studying legal theory (pursuing knowledge) and friends call inviting you to a party (friendship and play). The values pull in different directions. How do you decide?

The answer: basic commitments. We adopt a set of basic commitments that order the basic goods in our own lives. I may decide knowledge matters more to me at this point in my life than friendship — not because knowledge is objectively more important, but because I choose to give it priority now. Our commitments reflect how we see ourselves as persons and may change over time.

The limit: we should never adopt a life-plan that deliberately seeks to damage any basic value. Balancing study against friends is reasonable; setting out to destroy your friendships is not.

Finnis on utilitarianism

[5.200] Incommensurability leads Finnis to a strong critique of utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill), which holds that the moral worth of an action depends solely on maximising some overall measure of utility.

Utilitarians treat all moral reasoning as reducible to a single metric, so that in theory there is always a single right answer, obtainable by calculation. Finnis says this is wrong: sometimes there is no single right answer, because the basic values pull in different directions and more than one action may be compatible with them. It is a matter of which values you choose to emphasise at this point in your life.

His conclusion: reducing all moral decisions to a single metric is "not just impracticable, but … senseless" (Fundamentals of Ethics, p 87). Senseless in the same way as asking "which is greater, the length of my arm or the weight of your book?" You cannot compare length and weight; they are incommensurable.

The nine requirements of practical reasonableness

[5.210] Practical reasonableness is one of the seven basic goods, and also the method for pursuing all the others.

  • A coherent plan of life – adopt basic commitments and pursue them consistently; see your life as a whole with an overarching conception of what matters.
  • No arbitrary discounting or exaggeration – do not arbitrarily ignore or inflate any basic value; strike a reasonable balance.
  • No arbitrary preferences among persons – do not treat some people's interests as more valuable than others'. Everyone's basic commitments deserve the same respect.
  • Detachment – maintain objectivity about your projects so you stay open-minded about their importance.
  • Commitment – take your basic commitments seriously; do not abandon them lightly or on a whim.
  • Efficiency within reason – cost-benefit calculation is not irrelevant; be efficient, but efficiency is not the only or overriding factor.
  • Respect for every value in every act – never do any act that in itself is intended to damage or impede a basic value.
  • Having regard to the common good – act to promote the common good of your community.
  • Following one's conscience – in a sense this summarises the other eight. Never do anything you feel, all things considered, you ought not to do. Act in good faith.
Do not mix these up. Fuller has eight requirements of the internal morality of law. Finnis has nine requirements of practical reasonableness and seven basic values. Examiners test exactly this.

The common good

[5.220] For Finnis the common good is the interest everyone in a community has in creating an environment where all members can pursue the basic goods in their lives.

It is closely connected to community, which Finnis discusses through friendship: "friendship is the most communal, though not the most extended or elaborated form of human community" (NLNR, 2nd ed, 2011, p 143). The essence of friendship rests on a mutually supportive relationship — it is in my self-interest to have friends, but in a true friendship I also care about my friend for their own sake.

Subsidiarity

The interests of the community are subsidiary to the interests of the individuals who compose it. A community does not exist for its own sake. Its proper function is to assist individuals in pursuing the basic values and to help them coordinate their activities towards the objectives they have chosen.

Part 3: Finnis's Definition of Law

The focal meaning approach

Two ways to define law:

  • Lowest common denominator – find what every legal system shares. Produces a uselessly thin definition.
  • Central case (focal meaning) – identify the clearest, most complete example of law. Actual systems then resemble the central case to greater or lesser degrees.

Finnis takes the second route, selecting the practical point of law as the viewpoint: what legal institutions are for. Recall from Chapter 1 that this is his answer to Hart on whether description can be value-free.

The definition

The central case of law (NLNR, Ch 10, pp 276–277):

  • A set of rules
  • Made by a determinate and effective authority
  • In accordance with the rule of recognition (Hart's concept, borrowed)
  • Backed up by sanctions
  • Directed to pursuing the common good of the community

The first four elements are entirely positivist. Only the fifth is distinctively natural law. A rule with all five is law in the focal sense; a rule with only the first four is law in a weak or peripheral sense.

Part 4: Unjust Laws

Finnis does not hold that an unjust law is no law at all. He calls the strong thesis "pure nonsense" and self-contradictory. An unjust law is not law in the focal sense, but remains law in a weak sense if it satisfies the rule of recognition.

Part 5: Challenges to Finnis

The ethical status of animals

Finnis: animals lack the capacity to reflect on and rationally pursue the basic goods, so they have no rights.

Objection: the list was framed for humans in the first place. Why not ask what is inherently good for animals? Basic goods operate at intuitive as well as reflective levels — so why should only the reflective level count?

The "marital good"

Finnis posits marital intercourse between a man and a woman as a distinct basic good, unavailable to same-sex couples.

Objection: on his own theory same-sex couples can respect every basic good. The marital good appears driven by an emphasis on procreation, which is better understood as a dimension of the value of life than as a standalone basic value.

The global common good

If the common good is conceived globally, domestic laws that unreasonably privilege domestic interests at the expense of global justice lose moral force. Finnis is sympathetic but leaves this largely unexplored.

Legal authority

Finnis distinguishes laws that follow from the basic values by entailment (prohibiting murder) from pure coordination laws (which side of the road). He argues coordination requires authority because unanimous agreement is impractical.

Objection: many extremely complex coordination problems are solved by convention without any authority — natural languages being the obvious case. Finnis may overstate the necessity of authority.

The strong natural law thesis

Finnis dismisses "an unjust law is no law at all" as nonsense.

Objection: ordinary usage regularly diverges from technical meaning — "murder" in conversation is far broader than in the Crimes Act. Whether Finnis dismissed the strong thesis too quickly is genuinely arguable, and is a good essay topic.

Part 6: Mark C Murphy (b 1968)

Murphy is arguably the leading defender of natural law theory since Finnis, developing the tradition in new directions.
