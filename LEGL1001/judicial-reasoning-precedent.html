🏛️ Judicial Reasoning & the Doctrine of Precedent

LEGL1001 | Core Textbook Chapter Notes

1. The Elements of a Typical Appellate Judgment

The Story-Telling Element

  • Every law report starts with a story: a little human-interest story about two or more real people, one of whom has a grievance and wants a legal remedy
  • Though sometimes the parties may be corporations or the state, who may only represent the interests of real people at a remove
  • Most cases are from England (historical context). Like most cases that law students meet in their first few months
  • We do not learn every detail about the people and their stories, because not all of the facts are 'material' (relevant)
  • We have no idea how old either of the two people above are, nor what they looked like
  • The important question for the law is: what have the judges decided to do about the story?

The Practical Reasoning Element

  • Even as the parties have told their stories, the plaintiff (guided by a lawyer) will have been asking the court for a remedy based on rules of law thought to apply to the case
  • In each of these cases, and in any case which makes law worth studying, the defendant has denied that the law provides a remedy when such facts have occurred
  • Having retold the story, the judges will pose themselves a question: does the law allow a remedy in a fact-situation such as is revealed by the story?

The Parties' Arguments, and Issues

  • The reference to 'fact-situation' reminds us that judges are eventually supposed to decide a case in terms of a general rule, of which the outcome of the particular case will simply be an instance
  • Counsel will usually have argued in terms not simply of 'My client should win', but in terms of some general proposition, supposed to be based on precedents
  • The arguments will define some 'issues' of law: some questions of law that the parties are disputing about
  • In law reports that include summaries of counsel's argument, the issues should first be made clear in the summary of argument; otherwise they will be made evident, expressly or by implication, in the judges' reasons

2. Understanding What Judges Say: Facts, Decisions, Ratios and Dicta

An Express Statement of a General Rule

  • Sometimes the judges are good enough to state a rule in reasonable textbook form
  • Lord Atkin stated his conclusion as both the general 'neighbour' principle, and a more specific rule about careless manufacture
  • Among the three discussions of the seven issues in Carlill's case, there are some expressly stated rules too
  • However, the judgments in Carlill also offer many examples of the less clearly expressed ways of making a rule known

Application to the Facts Often Left Unstated

  • For logical tidiness, we would expect that in cases where the rule has been expressed in general 'If F then C' terms, the judges would spell out that in this case the facts are F and therefore the conclusion C must follow
  • Judges sometimes do this, but just as often do not bother
  • Example: In Donoghue v Stevenson, Lord Atkin simply says at the end 'I think that this appeal should be allowed'. It was not clear enough from what he had already said that Stevenson, the manufacturer (and indeed all negligent manufacturers), had just suffered a total defeat

Statements of 'Taken-for-Granted' Existing Law

  • Not everything in a judgment that looks like a statement of a legal rule is either ratio or obiter
  • As explained, the point of the essay-style written judgment is to justify the decision in terms of the existing law
  • Several or many rules of existing law may be restated in a judgment. If this is all that is happening to a rule (being restated without any attempt to explain difficulties in the earlier case or to 'reinterpret' it) we would not usually cite that rule as part of the ratio of the later case, but neither would we call it an obiter dictum
  • Example: Couldery v Bartrum (1880) 19 Ch D 394 (Jessel MR)
According to the English common law a creditor must accept anything in satisfaction of his debt except a less sum of money. He might take a horse, or a canary, or a tomtit if he chose... but he could not take 19s 6d in the pound.
  • This is a restatement of the law of contract (consideration), not the ratio of Couldery v Bartrum
  • On the other hand, sometimes a restatement of an old rule of law IS accepted as the ratio of a case when its status as law has been challenged (as the rule in Pinnel's case was in Foakes v Beer (1884) 9 App Cas 605)

3. Ratio Decidendi vs Obiter Dictum

What is an 'impression'?

  • The rule is supposed to be derived from previous cases
  • It should also be expressed so that it can be used to predict the outcome of future cases involving similar fact situations
  • We know several people who are now successful lawyers or law teachers who have confessed that they had studied law for a couple of years before they understood 'what they were looking for' when they read reports of cases
  • So it may be worth saying something about the contents of the typical judicial 'essay' in the law reports, and what to look for when reading it

Key Distinction

  • Ratio Decidendi = the legal rule or principle that is essential to the court's decision. It binds lower courts in the same hierarchy.
  • Obiter Dictum = any statement in a judgment that is NOT essential to the decision. May be persuasive but is not binding.
  • The difficulty: judges don't always label which is which. You must work it out from context, reasoning, and how later courts have treated the case.

4. The Level of Generality of a Ratio

  • So far, the above treatment of ratio decidendi and obiter dictum makes things seem simple
  • We expect to find express statements of law in judges' decisions, and if there is none we can infer a 'nice general statement of a rule'
  • But when we are looking for, or inferring, a 'nice general statement' of a rule, how general should it be?
  • If we were really in the mood to generalise, perhaps Donoghue v Stevenson and Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company stand for the proposition that the plaintiff always wins (or female plaintiffs always win!) Obviously this would be too general, as well as wrong.
  • It is impossible to answer the 'how general?' question in a few words or with a formula, and in the end the answer must always involve a degree of educated judgment: a 'feel' for the level of generality at which legal propositions are expressed

Factors in Determining the Level of Generality

  • For most cases, there is in the end a fairly obvious level of generality of the rule for which the case can be cited
  • But some cases present a real puzzle
  • To extract the ratio from the long written judgments in a law report would be one of the great challenges for developers of 'Artificial Intelligence' programs

The 'Cross Ratio' vs the 'Goodhart Ratio'

  • Professor Cross's definition: The ratio is a rule of law fairly close to the one stated in a textbook or in Halsbury's Laws of Australia. Read by itself, the case will lack context. A rule should be discernible, and the proper level of generality for stating it should be fairly obvious.
  • Professor Goodhart's approach: What later courts are really bound by is not any statement of a rule by the judge(s) in the case, but a proposition determined by taking account of the facts treated by the judge as material and his/her decision as based on those facts
  • Goodhart was insistent that readers of the case, or a judge in a later case, should not make their own decisions as to what facts are material, but should search the judgment for indications as to what the earlier judge thought to be material

How Different is the 'Goodhart Ratio' from the 'Cross Ratio'?

  • Cross & Harris say (at p 43) that 'there has been a high degree of unanimity among subsequent judges and writers' in regarding the 'manufacturer' passage in Donoghue v Stevenson as the ratio, and that the neighbour principle is NOT the ratio
  • The neighbour principle is best seen as an obiter of very high authority
  • Only in Chapter 10 will we need to worry more about just what the ratio of a case is, and how judges choose between alternative formulations of the ratio in the relatively rare cases where there are alternative formulations

5. Treatment of the Case in Later Cases and Texts

  • In some cases the uncertainty as to the correct level of generalisation remains even after one has considered the facts, the legal context and judges' reasons
  • Was the ratio of Wilkinson v Downton the broad one stated by Wright J, or the narrower one suggested first in Winfield's discussion of the case?
  • We can only state the ratio with confidence once we have seen how the case has been interpreted in later cases
  • The express statements in Wilkinson v Downton was broad enough to cover all intentional infliction of injury; you might therefore expect it to be cited and applied in cases of assault and battery, so that eventually these trespasses to the person would be subsumed under the broader head of the 'Wilkinson v Downton principle'. That has simply not happened, not yet anyway.

Consequences in this Book

  • The cases discussed in Part B provide the proof that usually it is clear enough what the ratio of a case is, and that in such cases it is the 'Cross ratio'
  • In the cases in that Part (except those in Chapter 10) there is little or no debate about what the ratio of some precedent is; the debate is: Does it bind that court, or is the court bound by the ratio of some other case, or free to reach its own decision?
  • However, in recognition of the fact that the ratio is not always a rule expressly stated by the judge(s), the co-author has adopted the following as a 'working definition' of the ratio:
The material facts of the case and the reasons for the decision that are essential or at least sufficient for the result in the case.

6. Some Further Points About Reading Cases

A Note on Headnotes

  • The editors of law reports kindly provide a 'headnote' at the top of each report
  • When they are good they can be very, very good; the student or practitioner in a hurry can find exactly what the ratio of the case is quickly and accurately without bothering to read the whole judicial essay
  • But consider: the headnote to Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co recites the facts and then says:
Held, affirming the decision of Hawkins J, that the above facts established a contract by the defendants to pay the plaintiff 100l in the event which had happened; that such contract was neither a contract by way of wagering within 8 & 9 Vict, c 109, nor a policy within 14 Geo 3, c 48, s 2; and that the plaintiff was entitled to recover.
  • That is, some headnotes are useless for understanding the resolution of the legal issues. It tells us of the outcome but nothing of the four or five points of contract law which the judgments established or restated
  • The only points of detail relate to the arguments that the judges dismissed in a sentence or so. Unfortunately this all-too-terse style is typical of the headnoting

Consequences for the Student

  • All this does raise a problem for the beginner. If we sometimes need to have a fairly complete knowledge of the pre-existing law, or the subsequent treatment of a case, before we can work out what its ratio is, how is the beginner to recognise that a case is saying something new, or that its apparent ratio has been confirmed by its treatment in later cases?
  • Well, of course, there will be cases where a beginner simply can't be expected to formulate the ratio as s/he will then find it in the textbooks, but in fact these are relatively rare
  • In most cases a first-year student should be able to pick up a case from any area of law (even something as specialised as, say, taxation law) and, by careful reading, work out what the issues were and state a ratio fairly close to the one stated in a textbook or in Halsbury's Laws of Australia
