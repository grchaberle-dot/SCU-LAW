Chapter: 3 – Classical Natural Law Theories

Key names: Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Augustine, Thomas Aquinas

What Is Natural Law?

Natural law theory holds that there is a necessary connection between law and morality: the existence or content of law depends in some way on its moral status.

The label "natural" points to the idea that moral standards are not invented by human choice. They are grounded in human nature, in reason, or in the objective good — and they are therefore discoverable rather than created.

The strong and weak theses

ThesisClaimProblem
StrongAn unjust law is not law at all (lex iniusta non est lex)Sounds absurd: courts plainly enforce unjust laws every day
WeakAn unjust law is law, but is defective as law — it fails to be law in the fullest senseHarder to attack, but risks becoming a merely verbal claim

Most modern natural law theorists, including Finnis, defend the weak thesis. Understanding this distinction is the single most valuable thing you can take from this chapter.

Natural law and religion

Natural law theory is historically entangled with Christian theology (Augustine and Aquinas were both theologians), but it is not logically dependent on religion. Aquinas himself argued that natural law is accessible through reason alone, without revelation. Finnis's modern version is presented in entirely secular terms.

Exam trap: never dismiss natural law as "just religion". That is a category error and markers punish it.

Socrates (c 470–399 BC)

Socrates wrote nothing. We know him through Plato's dialogues.

His death and the argument about legal obligation

Socrates was convicted by an Athenian jury of corrupting the youth and impiety, and sentenced to death by hemlock. He had the opportunity to escape and refused.

His reasoning (in the Crito) is the founding argument about the obligation to obey law:

  • He had lived his whole life under Athenian law and accepted its benefits.
  • By remaining in Athens when free to leave, he had tacitly agreed to abide by its laws.
  • Disobeying now, simply because the outcome went against him, would destroy the authority of law itself.
  • Therefore he must submit even to a verdict he believes is wrong.

This is the earliest version of social contract reasoning: obligation arises from consent, and consent can be implied from continued residence and acceptance of benefits.

The obvious objection: is staying somewhere really consent? Most people cannot realistically leave their country. Tacit consent arguments are still contested for exactly this reason.

Plato (c 428–348 BC)

The theory of Forms

Plato held that the world we perceive is not ultimate reality. Behind every particular thing lies a perfect, unchanging Form: the Form of Beauty, the Form of Justice, the Form of the Good. Particular just acts are just only because they participate in the Form of Justice.

The allegory of the cave

Prisoners chained facing a wall see only shadows cast by objects behind them, and take the shadows for reality. One prisoner escapes, sees the real objects and the sun, and returns to tell the others — who do not believe him.

The point: ordinary perception is shadow-knowledge. Philosophical understanding is difficult, painful, and unpopular, but it is the only route to truth. This is why Plato thinks philosophers, not the crowd, should rule.

The Good

The Form of the Good is the highest Form, the source of all other Forms and of knowledge itself — represented by the sun in the cave allegory.

The Republic and educative law

Plato's ideal state is ruled by philosopher-kings who have grasped the Forms. Crucially, for Plato law is not simply a system of coercion. Law has an educative function: its purpose is to make citizens virtuous, not merely to make them comply. Good law shapes character.

Aristotle (384–322 BC)

Plato's student, and the more empirically minded of the two.

Human nature and the political community

Aristotle holds that everything has a function (telos), and something is good when it fulfils its function well. The distinctive human function is rational activity. Human flourishing (eudaimonia) consists in exercising reason virtuously.

Humans are by nature political animals: we cannot flourish in isolation. The polis (political community) exists not merely so we can survive, but so we can live well. Law is what makes that possible.

Natural justice and legal justice

TypeDefinitionExample
Natural justiceValid everywhere, independent of what anyone enactsProhibition on murder
Legal justiceValid only because a community has decided itWhich side of the road to drive on

This is one of the most durable distinctions in the whole tradition. It reappears as Aquinas's ius gentium / ius civile split and again as Finnis's distinction between laws derived by entailment and laws of coordination.

Distributive and corrective justice

  • Distributive justice – the fair allocation of goods, honours and burdens across a community, proportionate to merit. Modern relevance: tax, welfare, resource allocation.
  • Corrective justice – restoring the balance when one person wrongs another, without regard to the parties' merit. Modern relevance: this is the backbone of tort and contract remedies.

Equity (epieikeia)

General rules are necessarily framed in general terms, and so will sometimes produce injustice in an unforeseen particular case. Equity is the corrective: it is a rectification of law where law is defective because of its generality.

Rule by law

Aristotle prefers the rule of law to the rule of individuals, because law is "reason unaffected by desire". Even a wise ruler is subject to passion; a general rule is not.

Augustine (354–430 AD)

Kingdoms and criminal gangs

Augustine's most quoted line: without justice, what are kingdoms but great criminal gangs? A band of robbers has leadership, rules, territory and a share-out of spoils. Strip justice away from a state and the only difference from a gang of thieves is scale.

The true commonwealth

Building on this, Augustine argues that a genuine res publica requires a shared commitment to justice. A political community held together only by power and common self-interest is not truly a commonwealth.

Unjust laws

Augustine gives the tradition its most famous slogan: a law that is unjust seems to be no law at all. This is the source of the strong natural law thesis — though note Augustine's careful "seems to be", which later writers often drop.

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)

The most systematic classical natural law theorist. Key text: Summa Theologiae.

Practical reason

Aquinas begins with the first principle of practical reason: good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided. This is self-evident and underived. Everything else in his moral theory builds from it. Finnis's self-evident basic values in Chapter 5 are a direct descendant of this move.

The four types of law

TypeWhat it is
Eternal lawGod's rational plan governing the whole of creation
Natural lawThe rational creature's participation in the eternal law — the part we can grasp through reason alone
Divine lawLaw revealed through scripture, supplementing what reason can reach
Human lawPositive law made by human authorities, which must be derived from natural law to be valid

Learn this table. It is the single most examinable item in the chapter.

Two ways human law derives from natural law

  • Ius gentium (conclusions) – derived from natural law by logical entailment. "Do not kill" follows directly from "do no harm". These principles are found across all legal systems.
  • Ius civile (determinations) – natural law underdetermines the answer, so the legislator makes a reasonable choice. Natural law says punish wrongdoers; it does not say six months. The specific figure is a determination.

Aquinas's definition of law

Law is an ordinance of reason, for the common good, made by the one who has care of the community, and promulgated. Four elements: reason, common good, proper authority, promulgation.

Unjust laws

Aquinas is subtler than the slogan suggests. An unjust law is a "perversion of law" and does not bind in conscience. But he adds a crucial qualification: one may still be obliged to comply in order to avoid scandal or civil disorder — that is, to avoid undermining the legal system as a whole.

He also distinguishes laws that are unjust because they are contrary to human good (which may be disobeyed) from laws contrary to divine good (which must be disobeyed).
