History and Language
In England prior to 1066, the language of the land was what is now known as English. Following the Battle of Hastings, William Duke of Normandy imposed Norman French. His successors, Henry II, brought Latin, the language of the church and scholarship. In 1731, Parliament banned French and Latin, forcing the use of English in courts. However, many Latin terms remained due to centuries of use. Modern legal writing movements, such as those led by Michael Kirby, advocate for the Ten Commandments of Plain English, including the preference for Germanic Anglo-Saxon roots over Latin or French backgrounds to ensure the law remains accessible to the laity.

Question 2310 marks

Negligence: The Neighbour Principle

In the landmark development of negligence law, the rule that 'you are to love your neighbour becomes in law, you must not injure your neighbour.'

Question for the Court: Who, then, in law is my neighbour?

The Answer: Persons who are so closely and directly affected by my act that I ought reasonably to have them in contemplation as being so affected when I am directing my mind to the acts or omissions which are called in question.

Analyse the Neighbour Principle as described above. Discuss the concept of Reasonable Foreseeability and how it limits the range of potential complainants in a negligence action.
