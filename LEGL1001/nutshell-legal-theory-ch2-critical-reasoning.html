The Structure of an Argument

[2.10] An argument is a set of statements where some statements (the premises) are offered as reasons to accept another statement (the conclusion).

An argument is not a disagreement or a fight. It is a structured piece of reasoning. Every legal submission you will ever write is an argument in this sense.

The skill to build: being able to take any messy paragraph and pull out (a) what is being claimed, and (b) what reasons are offered for it. Do this to your readings and to your own drafts.

Deductive Arguments

[2.20] A deductive argument is one where the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises. If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. There is no gap.

Classic form:

  • All humans are mortal. (premise)
  • Socrates is human. (premise)
  • Therefore Socrates is mortal. (conclusion)

Deduction does not add new information. It draws out what was already contained in the premises.

Validity and Soundness

[2.30] Two different tests, constantly confused by students.

TermTestConcerns
ValidDoes the conclusion follow from the premises?The structure of the argument only
SoundIs it valid and are all premises actually true?Structure and content

An argument can be perfectly valid but rubbish, because a premise is false:

  • All lawyers are honest. (false premise)
  • Georgia is a lawyer.
  • Therefore Georgia is honest. (valid form, unsound argument)
Attack strategy: when you critique an argument, decide first whether you are attacking its validity (the logic does not work) or its soundness (a premise is false). Saying "I disagree" without naming which is a weak answer.

Ampliative Arguments

[2.40] An ampliative argument is one where the conclusion goes beyond the information in the premises. It amplifies. The conclusion is not guaranteed even if all premises are true; it is only made more probable.

Most real-world and legal reasoning is ampliative, not deductive. Two main types: inductive and abductive.

Inductive Arguments

[2.50] Induction reasons from observed cases to a general rule, or from past patterns to future instances.

  • Every swan I have observed is white.
  • Therefore all swans are white.

Famously destroyed by the existence of black swans in Australia. Induction is always defeasible: a single counter-example can break it.

Legal relevance: reasoning from a line of decided cases to a general principle is inductive. That is why one distinguishable case can unsettle an apparently settled rule.

Abductive Arguments

[2.60] Abduction is inference to the best explanation. You observe some facts and conclude the explanation that best accounts for them.

  • The grass is wet.
  • Rain would explain that better than any alternative.
  • Therefore it probably rained.

Legal relevance: this is exactly what a court does with circumstantial evidence, and what you do when you argue that your reading of a statute best explains the section's purpose and context.

Descriptive and Normative Reasoning

[2.70] A descriptive statement says how things are. A normative statement says how things ought to be.

The is–ought gap, first pressed by David Hume (1711–1776) in A Treatise of Human Nature, Book III, Part I: you cannot validly derive an ought conclusion from purely is premises. Moral arguments often slide from describing what is the case to concluding what should be, and Hume's point is that this shift needs justifying, not smuggling.

This comes back in Chapter 5. Finnis's whole theory of self-evident basic values is built to dodge this objection: his values are not derived from facts, they are underived and indemonstrable.

Ethical Reasoning

[2.80] Normative arguments about law often rest on ethical premises. To evaluate them you need to identify the ethical theory in play (consequentialist? deontological? virtue-based?) and test whether its premises hold. Ethical theories get full treatment in Chapter 10.

Economic Reasoning

[2.90] Economic analysis evaluates legal rules by their consequences for efficiency and welfare: which rule produces the best allocation of resources, or the lowest total cost of accidents.

Strength: it is rigorous and makes trade-offs explicit. Weakness: it presupposes that all values can be reduced to a single measurable metric — which is precisely what Finnis attacks with his incommensurability argument in Chapter 5.

Public Policy and Law Reform Reasoning

Public policy reasoning asks what rule would best serve the community's interests, balancing competing objectives. Law reform reasoning goes further: it asks whether the existing rule should be changed, what should replace it, and what the transition costs are.

Good law reform arguments do four things: identify the defect in the current law, show the defect matters, propose a concrete alternative, and address the strongest objection to that alternative.

Tools of Critique

The standard moves for attacking an argument:

  • Deny a premise – show a stated premise is false.
  • Expose a hidden premise – identify an unstated assumption the argument needs, then attack it.
  • Attack the inference – accept the premises but show the conclusion does not follow.
  • Counter-example – produce a case where the premises hold but the conclusion fails.
  • Reductio ad absurdum – accept the argument temporarily and show it leads to an unacceptable conclusion.
  • Show the is–ought slide – identify where a descriptive premise was used to support a normative conclusion.

Legal Reasoning

Legal reasoning combines all of the above. A single judgment may contain deductive application of a statutory rule, inductive extraction of a principle from prior cases, abductive inference about the facts, and normative argument about policy.

The lawyer's skill is knowing which mode you are in at any moment, and not defending an inductive generalisation as if it were a deductive proof.
