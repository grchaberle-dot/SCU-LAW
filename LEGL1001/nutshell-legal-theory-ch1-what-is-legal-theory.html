Chapter: 1 – What Is Legal Theory?

Key names: H L A Hart, John Finnis, Thomas Hobbes

The Starting Point

[1.10] Legal theory is a branch of philosophy. The word philosophy literally means love of understanding (from the Greek philos, loving, and sophia, wisdom). Legal theory is therefore the attempt to genuinely understand law: not just to know what the rules say, but to grasp what law is, what it is for, and whether it deserves our obedience.

Legal theory is also called jurisprudence. The two terms are used interchangeably in most Australian law schools.

Why this matters for your degree: you cannot argue well about a legal rule if you do not understand what kind of thing a legal rule is. Theory is the toolkit behind every good legal argument.

The Popular View of Law

[1.20] Before doing any theory, it helps to notice what ordinary people already think about law. Most people, without ever studying jurisprudence, hold roughly three beliefs:

  • Law is a set of social rules. Law is not a private matter. It exists in and through a community, and it is publicly recognisable.
  • Law creates obligations. Legal rules are not merely predictions about what officials will do. They tell you what you ought to do, and people generally treat them as binding.
  • Law serves the common good. Law is supposed to be for the benefit of the community, not merely the benefit of whoever holds power.

Each of these popular beliefs turns out to be contested. The whole discipline of legal theory can be read as an argument about which of them survive scrutiny, and in what modified form.

Questions in Legal Theory

Legal theory asks two fundamentally different kinds of question, and confusing them is the single most common mistake in student essays.

TypeQuestion it asksExample
DescriptiveWhat is the case?What is law? What makes a rule legally valid?
NormativeWhat ought to be the case?Should we obey unjust laws? What should the law require?

Descriptive legal theory tries to explain law as it actually exists. Normative legal theory evaluates law against standards and says how it should be. Hart's project is primarily descriptive; much of natural law theory is at least partly normative.

Description and Evaluation

The neat descriptive/normative divide is harder to maintain than it first appears. Any description of law has to select a viewpoint. You cannot describe everything; you must decide which features matter.

Finnis's point: selecting which features of law are important is itself an evaluative judgment. To describe law well, you must first decide what law is for. Finnis therefore builds evaluation into his descriptive method by choosing the central case viewpoint (see Chapter 5).

Hart's response: Hart accepts that a theorist must choose a viewpoint, but he thinks you can adopt the viewpoint of participants in a legal system (the internal point of view) without endorsing it. You can describe how insiders see the law without agreeing that the law is good. This lets Hart claim his theory is descriptive and morally neutral.

Exam trap: do not say Hart "ignores morality". He explicitly accounts for how participants treat rules as morally binding. He just denies that a rule must be moral to be legally valid.

Law and Morality

The single biggest fault line in legal theory. Two camps:

  • Natural law theory – there is a necessary connection between law and morality. The existence or content of law depends in some way on its moral status. Covered in Chapters 3 and 5.
  • Legal positivism – there is no necessary connection. What counts as law is determined by social facts (who enacted it, whether officials accept it), not by whether it is just. Covered in Chapter 4.

Note carefully: positivists do not say law and morality never overlap, and they do not say unjust laws should be obeyed. They say the question "is this law?" and the question "is this just?" are two separate questions.

Law and Power

A third tradition sidesteps the law/morality debate entirely and asks instead: whose interests does law actually serve?

  • Thomas Hobbes – law is fundamentally an exercise of sovereign power. Without a sovereign to enforce order, life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short". Law is what makes social life possible, and it does so through coercion.
  • Critical legal theories – law is not the neutral instrument it presents itself as. Marxist, feminist, critical race and postcolonial theories all argue that law systematically advantages some groups over others while dressing that advantage up as objectivity and fairness.
