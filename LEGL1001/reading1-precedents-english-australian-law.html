The Courts

Authorised and Other Modern Reports

  • Reports are now collected and reprinted in the 176 volumes of the 'English Reports' (ER)
  • The importance of reliable court reporting was recognised when in 1865 there was set up in England a Council of Law Reporting charged with the responsibility of issuing official law reports
  • Private reports have been published in Australia (eg example, Wynes and Welsby's Supreme Court Reports in Victoria and the New South Wales Supreme Court Reports of Knox and Legge)
  • Such reports may be specialised (eg insurance and tax), or may comprise a selection of significant cases from various jurisdictions, as in the case of the Australian Law Reports and the All England Law Reports

Persuasive and Binding Precedent

The Development of Precedent

  • The use of precedents by barristers in argument and by judges in the course of handing down their decisions was a way of taking advantage of the experience and wisdom of others
  • For hundreds of years the use of precedents was regarded in that way: as a persuasive guide to the courts
  • But what had been a practice of convenience had turned, as the system of reporting cases developed, into a rule of law
  • By the late 19th century the concept of binding precedent (sometimes described as 'stare decisis') had emerged. It involved the principle that in certain circumstances the decisions of some courts would be binding on others, so that those later courts were deprived of the option of treating such decisions as merely persuasive and of rejecting them if the circumstances of the case warranted this

New Precedent Works

  • The operation of the doctrine of precedent may involve the consideration of two questions:
  • First: is the earlier decision one from a superior court whose decisions bind the court asked to apply that decision? A court may reject a decision from any other source (see §3.20–3.27)
  • Second: whether the circumstances of each case are such that the earlier decision binds the inferior court (see §3.38–3.39)

Which Courts Bind Other Courts?

Previous Decisions of Superior Courts Are Binding

  • The general rule is that the decision of a superior court in the same hierarchy of courts binds a lower court
  • A judge in a District or County Court is bound by a decision of the Supreme Court of their State (landed down as a case involving the same legal issue)
  • Similarly, a decision of an appellate bench of the High Court of Australia is binding on the Supreme Courts of the States, and of course on the courts below them
  • Courts are regarded as part of one hierarchy where linked by the right of appeal
  • Decisions of courts not forming part of the appeal hierarchy or a particular jurisdiction will not be binding on courts within that hierarchy

Previous Decisions of Other Inferior Courts Are Persuasive Only

  • The decisions of inferior courts such as Magistrates' and District Courts do not bind other such courts in the same hierarchy, even if higher in the criminal jurisdiction
  • Valentine v Eid (1992) 27 NSWLR 615: one practical reason for this is the fact that very few inferior court judgments are ever reported

Previous Decisions of the Same Court Are Strongly Persuasive

  • While judges in an inferior court will be bound by a decision of a superior court, they are not bound to follow the decisions of judges of the same inferior court if there are sufficiently strong reasons to justify departure from the earlier decision
  • That would occur where the earlier decision is considered to be clearly wrong: La Mantra v Minister for Primary Industries and Energy (1993) 110 ALR 201
  • An appellate court should exercise a 'strong conviction' as to the incorrectness of an earlier decision of that court before it would be appropriate to overrule that decision: Chatu Developments Pty Ltd v Barry (1989) 18 NSWLR 80

Decisions of Other Hierarchies Are Persuasive, Sometimes Strongly So

  • Do courts in one hierarchy ignore the decisions of other judicial systems?
  • Australian State courts sometimes ignore the decisions of other Australian State courts or of overseas tribunals such as English and American courts on the ground that they are not part of their own legal system
  • State courts: The first answer is that in the interests of legal certainty, a decision of one State court on a substantially similar issue will not be ignored by the courts of another State, despite the States being considered as separate legal systems. Such a decision will be regarded as of persuasive authority.
  • Such persuasive influence will be much stronger if the issue before the court involves the interpretation of legislation common to all the States: Australian Steamships Consolidated v Malbrough Goldmines Ltd (1993) 17 CLR 493

The Privy Council

  • The Privy Council: The High Court in Viro v R (1978) 18 ALR 257; 52 ALJR 418 held unanimously that it should no longer consider itself bound by decisions of the Privy Council
  • This attitude has been reinforced by the abolition of the Australian connection with the Privy Council
  • Acts (see §2.17) so that decisions of the Privy Council are now of persuasive influence only in Australian courts

When Will an Earlier Decision Be Applied?

  • The short answer to the second question stated at §3.19 (when will an earlier decision be applied) is that an inferior court is obliged to apply a decision of a superior court when the circumstances are such that the earlier decision cannot be 'distinguished' (see §3.29–3.30)
  • Even the situation must now reduce the courts: there must be a material similarity between the earlier authority and the case now to be decided (or one is used to bind the other)

This similarity is established by a process which:

  1. First identifies the legal principle, the ratio decidendi (see §3.29) which determined the earlier result
  2. Then involves identifying the facts considered relevant, ie the state they accounted for in applying their result, and
  3. Then determines whether the significant facts now before the court are sufficiently similar to the earlier situation to be caught by the ratio decidendi ('ratio' for short), governing that decision (if they are the court is bound by that decision if they can be distinguished it is not)

The High Court

  • In Australia the High Court has never followed the rule adopted by the House of Lords in 1898 (but abandoned in 1966) that a tribunal should be bound by its own previous decisions
  • However, the High Court will overrule its own previous decisions only with great caution: it is not enough that its majority consider a conclusion is preferred to the earlier decision (see Gosling Haulier Transport Commercialisation v Gold)
  • The matters to be weighed when the High Court considers overruling were discussed in John v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1989) 166 CLR 417. They took account of:
  • the earlier decision had been delivered by a Full Bench of the court after all relevant considerations, principles and authorities had been examined
  • no dissent or principle had been overlooked
  • the case had been fully argued
  • no later dissenting judgments had been delivered
  • no later decisions conflicted with the earlier decision
  • the decision could not be said to be manifestly wrong
  • the issues and the arguments put to the court in the instant case were the same as those previously considered

Other High Court Criteria for Overruling

  • A consideration of the inconvenience caused by the earlier decision
  • The time that has elapsed since the earlier decision
  • Whether the decision was unanimous
  • Whether majority judgments recorded differences in their reasoning
  • Whether that decision reflected a principle established in a significant stream of earlier decisions

Ratio Decidendi

  • It is a generally accepted principle that a court must give reasons for its decision, and it is difficult to escape from the duty to do so (though there is no such duty on juries)
  • A distinction is drawn between the ratio decidendi (the reason for deciding in a particular way) and other things said by the way; matter that do not form part of the reason for deciding the case
  • The ratio decidendi has been described by Halsbury Mawas as 'the principle or statement of law on which the previous decision is based to the extent to which it is essential in the decision'
  • It being recognised that there may be more than one ratio when the court assigns more than one ground for its decision: The Use and Abuse of Precedent (1988) 3 Jnl Bar Ass 91 at 101
  • The word 'essential' is important here. If the principle is stated more widely than is necessary to deal with the facts of the case, it will not be regarded as binding on subsequent courts
  • The reason for that particular rule in this legal argument is directed to the court on the facts of the case before the court
  • A statement of principle that extends to issues which have not been fully argued before the court should not be binding on other courts

Obiter Dicta

  • The obiter dicta are those observations which are not necessary to support the decision in question but form part of the judge's incidental reasoning or discussion
  • Although the obiter dicta of a superior court is not, technically speaking, binding on an inferior court, it may exercise extremely strong persuasive influence
  • Probably the best examples of such strongly persuasive obiter are to be found in the discussion of the 'negligence' question in the judgments of the Lords of Appeal who decided finally Byrne & Co v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465

Distinguishing

  • When lawyers talk about 'distinguishing' a previous case on its facts they are referring to the technique whereby a court rejects a previous decision by taking the view that the determinative facts of the previous case were not substantially similar to the significant facts in the present dispute
  • When a court accepts another court's view of the relevance of facts of a decision of that other court, but refuses to apply it because the relevant facts of the present case are considered to be different from those of the previous case, that is an ordinary example of 'distinguishing on the facts of the present decision'
  • Often the court does not do this, but takes its own narrower approach to the facts considered by it to have been relevant in the previous decision: this is referred to as 'restrictive distinguishing' (a reference to the restrictive read on the previous case)

Donoghue v Stevenson: Worked Example

Facts

  • The plaintiff went with a friend who purchased a bottle of ginger beer for her. The shopkeeper opened the bottle and poured the remainder of ginger beer out for the plaintiff
  • When she had drunk some of this her friend poured the remainder of the contents of the ginger beer bottle into the tumbler and a decomposed snail floated out
  • The result of this and of the impurities in the ginger beer which she had already drunk was that the plaintiff suffered shock and severe gastro enteritis

The Basic Question

The basic question for determination by the House of Lords was whether the manufacturer, who had no contractual relationship with this plaintiff, owed her any duty of care under that branch of the law of tort which deals with liability for negligence (see §14.22ff).

Reasoning

  • If the House of Lords had held as a matter of legal principle that the defendant owed a duty of care to the plaintiff simply because of the fact that he was the manufacturer of the goods, this would have resulted in a very wide principle of liability for manufacturers
  • The manufacturer's making of the goods in question would be sufficient to establish the existence of a duty of care to the ultimate consumer
  • However, the court considered the relevant facts: the ginger beer was contained in an opaque bottle sealed with a metal cap which could not be readily removed or replaced
  • The legal principle involved would be narrowed in operation, because it would mean that the fact of manufacture was not of itself sufficient to give rise to the duty of care
  • This duty of care would, in those circumstances, exist where the manufacturer presented his goods in a way that made inspection impossible before consumption

The Scope of the Duty

When passing the question raised by this case in the opening passage of his judgment, Lord Atkin referred to the liability of the manufacturer of an article of food or a person who manufactures or puts up for sale (in packages so made as to preclude the possibility of intermediate examination) a medicine, poison, etc. The ultimate consumer who was injured therefore could recover damages from the manufacturer or vendor.

Being discussed would be further reduced, as it would now be regarded as applying to the manufacturers of foodstuffs only and not to manufacturers generally.

When Will Overruling Occur?

  • It is appropriate, after discussing the circumstances in which one court is bound by another's decision, to examine the circumstances in which appellate courts will consider overruling (that is, rejecting as wrongly decided) their own previous decisions
  • In Australia the High Court has never followed the rule, adopted by the House of Lords in 1898 but abandoned in 1966, that a tribunal should be bound by its own previous decisions
  • The High Court will overrule its own previous decisions only with great caution
  • The matters to be weighed were discussed in Territorial Harbour Board v Scottish Sales Line Ltd (1914) 18 CLR 306 and more recently in John v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

The Role of Law Reform Commissions

  • The pressures generated by the accelerating rate of social and commercial change and by the haphazard development of case law have been influential in the establishment in both State and Commonwealth levels of law reform commissions
  • These bodies are set up by statute, are staffed by lawyers who may have regard to any relevant professional opinion as well as the views of the public
  • Their function is to examine defects in specified areas of the law and to recommend appropriate legislative reforms
  • Their resources have been significantly reduced in recent years (see Peter Hanburied, 'The Changing Face of Law Reform' (1999) 73 ALJ 50)

Certainty vs Flexibility

  • Certainty is desirable in any legal system: citizens are entitled to know their rights and to fulfil their obligations and to be able to make decisions knowing, broadly, what the legal consequences will be
  • On the other hand, a society not static: its values, its aspirations change and this creates the need for changes in the law
  • A legal system that is to survive must meet the needs generated by social change
  • Legislation provides an answer to the need for large-scale comprehensive changes but is too general and occasionally a form of horse-trading to satisfy completely all the needs for change
  • It is here that the judiciary plays an important role: one made possible by the overruling process

In essence, stare decisis as an operation in national policy which calls for an assurance of a 'society of liberty in which, subject between the rich for certainty, consistency and predictability against the competing need for justice, flexibility and rationality'
