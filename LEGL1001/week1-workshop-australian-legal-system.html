Week 1 Workshop: Researching & Understanding the Australian Legal System

The Australian Legal System

  • Common law system based on English Law
  • Commonwealth Constitution divides power between Commonwealth and States/Territories
  • Separation of Powers: Legislature (Parliament), Judiciary (Judges), Executive (Government)
  • Sources of Law: Legislation, Case Law, International Law, Indigenous Customary Laws

Case Law: Court Hierarchy

  • State/Territory: Supreme Court > Intermediate Courts (County) > Lower Court (Magistrates) > Civil & Admin Tribunals
  • Federal: Federal Court/FCFCA Division > FCFCA Division 2 > AAT & other Tribunals
  • Ultimate court of appeal for both: High Court of Australia

Doctrine of Precedent

  • Precedent = legally binding nature of earlier court decisions
  • All High Court rulings are binding. Same hierarchy = binding. Different hierarchies = persuasive only.
  • Ratio Decidendi = The legal principle upon which the decision is founded. Binds courts at same level or below.
  • Obiter Dictum = Remarks in passing, not essential to decision. Persuasive only.
  • Stare Decisis = Court bound to follow previous decisions unless inconsistent with High Court or wrong in law.

Weight of Persuasive Decisions

  • Higher court = more significant
  • Unanimous > dissenting judgements
  • More current > old decision
  • Criticised decision = less significant

Application

  • Superior court in same hierarchy? YES = bound (unless distinguishable). NO = not binding but may be persuasive.
  • Judge may: follow, distinguish, apply, or overrule
  • Legislation overrides common law

Judgements

  • Single: one judgement for the court
  • Joint: more than one judge signs
  • Plurality: separate judgements, same conclusion
  • Dissenting: minority; disagrees with majority

Finding the Ratio

  1. Read catchwords and headnote
  2. Read entire decision
  3. Find statement of principles of law

Case Structure

  • Headnote: summary by publisher (NOT by judge, NOT part of decision)
  • Includes: facts, question of law, decision, precedents, litigation history, counsel arguments
  • Unreported cases still have precedent value; online databases make them accessible

Legislation

  • Law created by state, territory and federal parliaments
  • Statute law (Acts) + delegated legislation (regulations, by-laws)
  • Commencement: new law takes effect once commenced; remains until repealed
  • Can be amended and reprinted/consolidated at any time

Delegated Legislation

  • Responsibility of administrative arm of government
  • Forms: regulations, rules, orders, by-laws, ordinances, proclamations, notices
  • Takes effect when gazetted; parent Act contains enabling provisions

Structure of an Act

Number, Date, Reprint/compilation, Table of contents, Long title, Short title, Simplified outline, Preamble, Sections, Chapters/Parts/Divisions, Headings, Schedules

Hierarchy: Chapters > Parts > Divisions > Subdivisions > Sections > Subsections > Paragraphs > Subparagraphs

Statutory Interpretation

Traditional Approaches

  • Literal: plain meaning rule (even if ridiculous)
  • Golden Rule: ordinary meaning unless absurd/repugnant
  • Purposive/Mischief: find the wrong Parliament tried to correct

Modern Approach (s 15AA)

"The interpretation that would best achieve the purpose or object of the Act is to be preferred."

  • Courts may use intrinsic materials (within Act: title, preamble, headings, schedules)
  • Courts may use extrinsic materials (outside Act: parliamentary debates, committee reports, international agreements) per s 15AB
  • Other: repealed statutes, Acts in pari materia, amending Acts, delegated legislation

Other Sources of Law

Common Law Countries

England, Canada, India, USA, NZ. Influential but not binding on Australian courts. Eg High Court used Canadian decisions in Mabo (No 2).

Civil Law Countries

Italy, France. All law as codes (not case law). Influential for legislation development.

Customary Law

First Nations laws gaining recognition. Never ceased to operate. Oral tradition; permission required to access.

Secondary Sources

Not law. Textbooks, encyclopaedias, journal articles, legal dictionaries. Used to understand and find primary sources.
