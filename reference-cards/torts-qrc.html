Torts | Prue Vines | April 2021

Legislation

  • CLA = Civil Liability Act; WrA = Wrongs Act; CL(W)A = Civil Liability (Wrongs) Act; PI(LD)A = Personal Injuries (Liabilities and Damages) Act
  • 'CLA modifies' = major CL legislation modifies common law position
  • 'CLA confirms' = major CL legislation confirms common law position
  • Major CLA legislation passed 2002 onwards in all jurisdictions

What Is a Tort?

  • A civil wrong, distinguished from contract, restitution and equitable wrongs
  • This card covers: negligence, wrongful death, breach of statutory duty, trespass, intentional torts, defamation, nuisance
Action in TrespassAction in Case
DirectnessDirect: Scott v Shepherd (1773)Indirect
DamageNo need to prove (actionable per se, except possibly trespass to goods)Must prove damage
IntentIntentional/unintentionalIntentional/unintentional
OnusOn defendant (lack of fault)On plaintiff (fault)

Examples trespass: assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass to land/goods

Examples case: negligence, nuisance, defamation, conversion, misfeasance in public office, intentional infliction of personal injury, deceit, malicious prosecution

Negligence

  • Common law tort; action on the case. Damage must be proved.
  • Elements: duty owed by D to P; duty breached by D; breach causing damage to P (not too remote)

Duty

  • D owes duty if reasonably foreseeable that if D carries out act/omission, a person in P's position could be harmed ('neighbour principle'): Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] 1 AC 562
  • Salient factors for reasonable foreseeability: Sullivan v Moody (2001) 207 CLR 562; Caltex Refineries v Stavar (2009) 75 NSWLR 649
  • Not necessary for precise chain of events to be foreseeable, merely that final outcome is reasonably foreseeable: Chapman v Hearse (1961) 106 CLR 112; Palsgraf v Long Island Railroad Co (US)

No Duty Situations

  • Unforeseeable plaintiff: Sydney Water Corporation v Turano (2009) 239 CLR 51
  • Barristers/solicitors whose work is 'intimately connected' with court work

Categories of Duty

  • Omissions: No general duty to rescue: Hargrave v Goldman (1963) 110 CLR 40. Exceptions: prison officers, parents, schools/employers, person who knows of hazard and could reduce care, person who creates a danger, rescuer
  • Pure economic loss by act: duty where damage/injury to P is specifically foreseeable, or P is in specifically foreseeable class: Perre v Apand Pty Ltd (1999) 198 CLR 180
  • Pure economic loss by negligent misstatement: D assumes responsibility and P reasonably relies: Hedley Byrne & Co Ltd v Heller & Partners Ltd [1964] AC 465
  • Nervous shock (psychiatric injury): common law duty arises where reasonably foreseeable P would suffer psychiatric harm: Tame v New South Wales; Annetts v Australian Stations (2002) 211 CLR 317. CLA modifies: normal fortitude required (all except NT and Qld)
  • Children: Action crystallises when child born alive: Watt v Rama [1972] VR 353. Mother can owe duty to unborn: Lynch v Lynch (1991) 25 NSWLR 411
  • Occupiers: General duty owed to visitors: Australian Safeway Stores v Zaluzna (1987) 162 CLR 479
  • Public authorities: treat as ordinary defendant. Salient factors: nature of statutory functions, level of control and knowledge, vulnerability
  • Product liability: manufacturer owes duty to ultimate consumer: Donoghue v Stevenson; Grant v Australian Knitting Mills [1936] AC 85

Breach of Duty

  • CLA: no breach unless risk is foreseeable and not insignificant and a reasonable person would take precautions
  • Basic test: did D act like a reasonable person in the circumstances: Wyong Shire Council v Shirt (1980) 146 CLR 40
  • Standard of care = 'calculus of negligence': (i) probability of harm: Paris v Stepney Borough Council [1951] AC 367; (ii) gravity: Paris; (iii) utility of action: Watt v Hertfordshire County Council [1954] 1 WLR 835; (iv) practicability of precautions: Romeo v Conservation Commission of NT (1998) 192 CLR 431
  • Children: standard of ordinary child of same age: McHale v Watson (1966) 115 CLR 199
  • Learners: standard of ordinary person in circumstances (eg learner driver meets standard of ordinary driver): Imbree v McNeilly (2008) 82 ALJR 1374
  • Professionals: standard of ordinary skilled person professing that skill: Phillips v William Whiteley Ltd [1938] 1 All ER 566. CLA modifies*: no breach if professional acted in way accepted by peer professional opinion as competent practice, unless court considers that opinion irrational

Evidence of Breach

  • Onus of proof of fault lies on P
  • Judge and jury determine standard; res ipsa loquitur allows inference (not a legal presumption): Schellenberg v Tunnel Holdings (2000) 200 CLR 121

Causation

  • General test: 'but for' plus common sense and value judgment: March v Stramare (1991) 171 CLR 506
  • CLA requires: 'cause in fact' (breach is necessary condition of harm) AND whether scope of liability extends to harm: various CLA sections
  • Adeels Palace v Moubarak (2009) 239 CLR 420: first stage should not involve value judgment; 'but for' should be used except where exceptional circumstances provision applies
  • CLA: where suggested cause cannot be established as necessary condition, court determines whether and why responsibility should be imposed on D
  • Loss of chance: not available in Australia: Tabet v Gett (2010) 240 CLR 537
  • Increased risk: material contribution may be sufficient: Fairchild v Glenhaven Funeral Services [2003] 1 AC 32; CL(W)A (ACT) s 45(2)

Remoteness

  • Harm must not be too remote from breach of duty
  • Foreseeability as to kind of harm required: Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Morts Dock (Wagon Mound No 1) [1961] AC 388
  • Not the manner of occurrence (Jolley v Sutton London Borough Council [2000] 3 All ER 409) nor extent ('eggshell skull' rule): Stephenson v Waite Tileman Ltd [1973] 1 NZLR 152
  • Explicit in CLA as 'scope of liability' in all jurisdictions except Tas

Defences to Negligence

Contributory Negligence

  • P's failure to take reasonable care for own safety: Astley v Austrust Ltd (1999) 197 CLR 1
  • Was complete defence at common law; now damages apportioned according to degree of fault (all jurisdictions)
  • CLA provides standard same as for breach: Boral Bricks v Cosmidis (No 2) [2014] NSWCA 139
  • In all jurisdictions except Vic: P presumed contributorily negligent where intoxicated or when injured in motor accident having failed to wear seatbelt
  • CLA* allows 100% contributory negligence

Voluntary Assumption of Risk

  • Volenti non fit injuria: complete defence (but no longer for motor vehicle cases except for racing in NSW)
  • P voluntarily undertook the risk and actually knew of the particular risk: Scanlon v American Cigarette Company (Overseas) [1987] VR 289
  • Volenti does not apply in motor vehicle accidents where contributory negligence is presumed
  • At common law this is a complete defence. CLA* modifies: presumption that P would be aware of 'obvious risks'

Dangerous Recreational Activity

  • D not liable for obvious risk arising from a dangerous recreational activity: CLA in NSW (s 5F), Qld (s 19), WA (s 5H)
  • 'Dangerous recreational activity' = significant risk of physical harm: Fallas v Mourlas (2006)

Illegality

  • P committing unlawful act is not a defence per se
  • Where P engaged in joint illegal enterprise with D, whole relationship considered: Miller v Miller [2011] HCA 9
  • Statutory illegality defences in all jurisdictions except Vic and WA (CLA*): P's conduct must be criminal offence of requisite severity and offence contributed to harm

Intentional Torts

Trespass to the Person

  • Requires a positive act: Innes v Wylie (1844) 174 ER 800
  • In Australia, trespass may be negligent or intentional: Williams v Milotin (1957) 97 CLR 465

Assault

  • Intentionally and directly inflicting reasonable apprehension of immediate physical harm: Zanker v Vartzokas (1988) 34 A Crim R 11
  • Words alone may suffice if threat not immediate but has been found to arise by telephone: Barton v Armstrong [1969] 2 NSWLR 451

Battery

  • Directly (Scott v Shepherd (1773)) and intentionally or negligently or recklessly touching P: Collins v Wilcock [1984] 1 WLR 1172
  • Contact must be offensive beyond normal usage of daily life: Rixon v Star City Pty Ltd (2001) 53 NSWLR 98

False Imprisonment

  • Directly and intentionally or negligently confining P within a fixed area without legal authority: Balmain New Ferry Co v Robertson (1906) 4 CLR 379
  • P need not know they have been imprisoned: Murray v Ministry of Defence [1988] 1 WLR 692
  • Must be no reasonable means of escape: Bird v Jones (1845) 115 ER 911

Trespass to Goods/Chattels

  • P must have possession at time of interference: Penfold Wines Pty Ltd v Elliott (1946) 74 CLR 204
  • Interference must be direct; negligent interference may be sufficient: McHale v Watson
  • Applies to tangible chattels only: Hoath v Connect Internet Services (2006) 229 ALR 566

Trespass to Land

  • P must have exclusive possession at time of trespass: Hines v Hines [1999] QCA 149
  • 'Land' includes soil, subsoil, fixtures and airspace to extent land can be enjoyed: Lord Bernstein of Leigh v Skyviews [1978] QB 479
  • D must intend/be reckless or negligent in causing physical contact with land: Public Transport Commission (NSW)

Conversion

  • Deliberate dealing with goods inconsistent with rights of owner: Kuwait Airways v Iraqi Airways (Nos 4 & 5) [2002] 2 AC 883
  • Remedy: forced sale of chattel to D; value assessed at date of judgment

Deceit

  • Elements: Derry v Peek (1889); Magill v Magill (2006) 226 CLR 551
  • False representation to P; made by D knowing false or reckless as to falsity; inducing and intending P to rely; causing loss to P

Malicious Prosecution

  • D initiates legal process against P; process terminates in P's favour; D acted maliciously without reason and probable cause; P shows damage: A v New South Wales [2007] HCA 10

Defences to Intentional Torts

  • Consent: must be real and freely given: Department of Health and Community Services (NT) v JWB (Marion's case) (1992) 175 CLR 218. May be express or implied. Only advised in broad terms of nature of interference: Rogers v Whitaker (1992) 175 CLR 479. P must have capacity.
  • Necessity: protection of property (eg dismantling house to stop fire) or person (medical emergency). Does not override competent adult's refusal: Re B (Adult: Refusal of Medical Treatment) [2002] 2 All ER 449
  • Self-defence: D must show reasonable apprehension and force not greater than reasonably necessary: Fontin v Katapodis (1962) 108 CLR 177. CLA modifies in NSW; extended from criminal law in NT, Qld, Tas, WA
  • Provocation: not a defence to battery/assault at common law, but may be extended in Qld and WA

Defamation

  • Arises when false statements lower P's reputation: Radio 2UE Sydney v Chesterton (2009) 238 CLR 460
  • Test: whether a sensible reader would reasonably think P was referred to: Morgan v Odhams Press [1971] 1 WLR 1239
  • Statement published by D to at least one other person than P
  • Uniform Defamation Laws (UDLs): Defamation Act 2005 (NSW) and equivalents modify common law
  • Distinction between libel (written) and slander (oral) abolished
  • Corporations cannot sue unless fewer than 10 employees or not-for-profit
  • Common law defences + statutory defences; remedies include injunction and damages
  • Defences: justification, public benefit/interest, absolute privilege, qualified privilege, fair comment

Nuisance

Public Nuisance

  • Interference with a public right. Action by Attorney-General or private person proving particular damage: Walsh v Ervin [1952] VLR 361
  • D's interference must be substantial and unreasonable: State Government Insurance Commission v Trigwell (1979) 142 CLR 617

Private Nuisance

  • Indirect interference with private right of enjoyment of land
  • P must prove title to sue; must be actual possessor (not mere licensee): Hunter v Canary Wharf [1997] AC 655
  • Interference must be substantial and unreasonable (objective test), not abnormal sensitivity: Robinson v Kilvert (1889) 41 Ch D 88
  • May be tangible (vibration, flooding) or intangible (smells, pollution, noise): Victoria Park Racing v Taylor (1937) 58 CLR 479
  • Coming to the nuisance is NOT a defence: Sturges v Bridgman (1879) 11 Ch D 852
  • Remedies: damages, injunction, abatement. Now often by statute (eg Trees between Neighbours)

Concurrent Liability & Vicarious Liability

Concurrent Liability

  • Occurs where multiple tortfeasors liable for same damage (joint conduct, vicarious liability, non-delegable duty, multiple torts causing same damage)
  • At common law CL was solidary: P could recover whole damage from one of the concurrently liable Ds
  • Most jurisdictions have legislated for proportionate liability for negligently caused property damage and pure economic loss (see CLA)

Vicarious Liability

  • May be VL of principal for agent, employer for employee, partner for partner
  • Typically liability of employer for employee's wrong; principal not liable for independent contractor: Sweeney v Boylan Nominees (2006) 226 CLR 161
  • Requirements: P must show tortfeasor committed a tort; P must show requisite relationship (employer-employee); use multi-factorial test: Hollis v Vabu (2001) 207 CLR 21
  • Wrong must be committed during employment and closely enough connected to work: New South Wales v Lepore (2003) 212 CLR 511

Non-Delegable Duty

  • Where D regarded as having personal or non-delegable duty, delegation insufficient to discharge: Northern Sandblasting v Harris (1997) 188 CLR 313
  • Arises where P especially vulnerable and D has power: hospital to patient, school to child, employer to employee

Wrongful Death

  • Family members of deceased can sue for wrongful death where victim could have sued for wrongful act, neglect or default had they not died (Lord Campbell's Act)
  • Compensation limited to expectations of financial support and funeral expenses
  • Various legislation in each jurisdiction

Breach of Statutory Duty

  • Separate tort: Darling Island Stevedoring v Long (1957) 97 CLR 36. Not to be confused with negligence where breach involves violation of statute
  • For public authorities, CLA modifies*
  • Elements: Parliament intended private right of action for damages for breach; duty imposed on D: Cutler v Wandsworth Stadium Ltd [1949] AC 398
  • Indications: statute is to benefit a particular class (Phillips v Britannia Hygienic Laundry [1923] 1 KB 539); right of action most likely available where specific rather than general duties

Damages

  • Compensatory: put P back in position if tort had not happened: Livingstone v Rawyards Coal Co (1880) 5 App Cas 25. Lump sum discounted for present value; assessed when cause of action is complete
  • Exemplary/punitive: to punish D (limited/abolished by CLA but may be available otherwise)
  • Aggravated: compensating for humiliation or emotional distress due to D's malicious/insulting manner: Cassell & Co v Broome [1972] AC 1027
  • Nominal: where torts actionable per se (right infringed but no other damage)
  • Contemptuous: court awards lowest coin as signal bare claim should not have been brought

Personal Injury Damages

  • Economic loss: lost earnings (past and future); loss of earning capacity; gratuitous services (past and future): Griffiths v Kerkemeyer (1977) 139 CLR 161; loss of capacity to provide services to others: Sullivan v Gordon (1999) 47 NSWLR 319; CSR v Eddy [2005] HCA 64
  • Non-economic loss (general damages): pain and suffering; loss of amenity (enjoyment of life): Sharman v Evans (1977) 138 CLR 563; lost expectation of life
  • CLA statutory thresholds and caps* for non-economic loss. Thresholds on general damages in some jurisdictions
  • Collateral sources (benefits) set off against damages depending on nature/intention of benefit: National Insurance Co of NZ v Espagne (1961) 105 CLR 569

Survival of Actions

  • Except re defamation and seduction, actions survive (relevant legislation in administration and probate Acts*)
  • Where D dies: if action commenced, no defence bars survival; if not commenced, survival depends on whether cause arose before death*
  • Where P dies: P's estate can bring action if P would have been able; can sue for loss of P's earning capacity and economic loss between injury and death, plus funeral expenses
