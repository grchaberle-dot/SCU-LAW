Statutory Interpretation | Tristan Taylor | April 2020

Basic Concepts

  • Two forms of legislation: statutes/Acts (Parliament) and delegated/subordinate legislation (delegated bodies)
  • Forms of delegated legislation: regulations, statutory rules, legislative instruments, by-laws
  • Legislation is proactive; case law is reactive
  • Validly enacted legislation prevails over inconsistent case law

Process of Making Statutes

  1. Proposal for a bill through political process
  2. Drafting of detailed instructions (responsible Minister)
  3. Preparation of draft bill by parliamentary counsel
  4. Placement on Notice Paper
  5. First reading (presentation to Clerk)
  6. Second reading speech (purpose and contents)
  7. Second reading debate
  8. Committee consideration
  9. Third reading
  10. Vote
  11. If bicameral: repetition in other chamber
  12. Governor-General/Governor approval

Approaches to Interpretation

Literal Approach

  • Words interpreted according to plain and ordinary meaning: Engineers' Case (1920) 28 CLR 129
  • Now recognised literal meaning may yield to purpose and context: Project Blue Sky v Australian Broadcasting Authority (1998) 194 CLR 355

Golden Rule

  • Depart from plain meaning to avoid absurd/inconsistent result: Grey v Pearson (1857) 6 HL Cas 61

Purposive Approach

  • Determine Parliament's purpose and adopt interpretation consistent with it
  • Origins: Re Heydon's Case (1584) 3 Co Rep 7a

'Modern Approach'

  • Context considered at first instance (not only when ambiguous)
  • 'Context' in widest sense: existing state of law + mischief Act intended to address
  • Key: CIC Insurance Ltd v Bankstown Football Club Ltd (1997) 187 CLR 384
  • Recent: R v A2; R v Magennis; R v Vaziri [2019] HCA 35

Purposive Approach Under Legislation

Cth: Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s 15AA: interpretation that best achieves the purpose or object is to be preferred.

States: Legislation Act 2001 (ACT) s 139; Interpretation Act 1987 (NSW) s 33; Acts Interpretation Act 1954 (Qld) s 14A; Interpretation of Legislation Act 1984 (Vic) s 35(a); Interpretation Act 1984 (WA) s 18

Extrinsic Aids

Materials outside the legislation that supply context:

  • Parliamentary debates: Wacando v Commonwealth (1981) 148 CLR 1
  • Constitutional materials: Cole v Whitfield (1988) 165 CLR 360
  • Reports of inquiries: Barker v R (1983) 153 CLR 338
  • International agreements: Enzed Holdings v Wynthea Pty Ltd (1984) 4 FCR 450

Cth s 15AB: Use of Extrinsic Materials

  • (a) To confirm ordinary meaning in light of context/purpose
  • (b) To determine meaning where ambiguous/obscure OR produces absurd result

Under Modern Approach

  • Law reform reports: CIC Insurance v Bankstown Football Club
  • Explanatory memoranda: Newcastle City Council v GIO General Ltd (1997) 191 CLR 85
  • Second reading speeches: Burch v South Australia (1998) 71 SASR 12
  • But: primacy of text affirmed: R v Getachew (2012) 248 CLR 22

Context Rules

  • Ejusdem generis: general expression after specific list read down in light of specifics
  • Noscitur a sociis: meaning from surrounding words: R v Ann Harris (1836)
  • Expressio unius est exclusio alterius: express mention may exclude others
  • All words carry meaning: Project Blue Sky at [71]
  • Words interpreted with current meaning: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Clark (2003)
  • Consistent use of words assumed (but may be displaced)
  • Dictionaries may be consulted but must give way to context
  • Provisions interpreted with reference to other legislation (in pari materia)
  • Reference to audience: Collector of Customs v Agfa-Gevaert Ltd (1996) 186 CLR 389

Presumptions

Courts interpret against common law presumptions. Rebuttable by clear and unambiguous words: Durham Holdings v NSW (2001) 205 CLR 399

  • No retrospective operation: Yrttiaho v Public Curator (Qld) (1971) 125 CLR 228
  • No interference with fundamental rights: Coco v R (1994) 179 CLR 427
  • No abrogation of self-incrimination privilege: Crafter v Kelly [1941] SASR 237
  • No deprivation of court access: Plaintiff S157/2002 v Cth (2003) 211 CLR 476
  • Re-enactment approves prior interpretation: Public Service Assoc v Industrial Commission of NSW (1985)
  • Does not bind the Crown: Bropho v WA (1990) 171 CLR 1
  • Penal provisions strictly construed: Beckwith v R (1976) 135 CLR 569; but modern: Alcan (NT) v Commissioner of Territory Revenue (2009) 239 CLR 27
  • Property rights not taken without compensation
  • No extraterritorial effect: Jumbunna Coal Mine v Victorian Coal Miners' (1908) 6 CLR 309
  • Conformity with international law

Precedent & Statutory Interpretation

  • Doctrine of precedent applies. Courts higher in hierarchy bind those below.
  • High Court not bound by own decisions but departs with reluctance: John v Federal Commissioner for Taxation (1989) 166 CLR 417
  • 'Plainly erroneous': Babaniaris v Lutony Fashions (1987) 163 CLR 1
  • Uniform national legislation: intermediate appellate courts should not depart unless 'plainly wrong': Australian Securities Commission v Marlborough Gold Mines (1993) 177 CLR 485

Practical Checklist

  1. Was legislation in force at the relevant date?
  2. Has it been amended since? Were changes retrospective?
  3. Use table of contents/headings to find relevant provisions
  4. Read carefully, noting significant words/phrases
  5. Break provision into elements (criteria)
  6. Check if words/phrases are defined in the legislation
  7. Check dictionary if not defined in Act
  8. Consider interpretation legislation provisions
  9. Check adjacent provisions
  10. Check if judicially interpreted
  11. Interpret according to ordinary/technical meaning
  12. Identify purpose and interpret consistently
  13. If ambiguous/absurd: use parliamentary and related materials
  14. If not: use extrinsic materials under CIC Insurance principle
  15. Interpret against relevant common law presumptions
