Administrative Law | Robin Creyke | January 2022

Information Rights: FOI

  • FOI assists a person to obtain information about factors informing an administrative decision
  • All Australian jurisdictions have FOI laws: eg Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth)
  • Government information is a national resource; FOI promotes accountability and open government
  • General right of access to documents: Pt IV s 11. No need to provide a reason or have standing: s 11(2)
  • Anyone can apply for documents of an agency or 'official documents' of a Minister: s 11(1)
  • 'Agency' = government department, prescribed authority or Norfolk Island Authority: s 4(1)

Obtaining a Document

  • Agencies may provide access under 'administrative access' or informal release: s 3A
  • Formal application: request in writing, describe document, how to notify applicant: s 15
  • Body must acknowledge and decide on access within 30 days: s 15
  • Access can be given to inspect or copy; may be refused with reasons: ss 20, 26

Limitations & Exemptions

  • Right enforceable only if agency has physical or constructive possession: s 4(1); Beesley v AFP
  • Document must be in existence (can't require creation)
  • Part IV exemptions and 'conditional exemptions': s 11A(4)-(6)
  • Exemptions include: national security/defence (s 33), international relations (s 33), law enforcement/public safety (s 37), Cabinet (s 34), material obtained in confidence (s 45)
  • 'Conditional exemptions': documents where release contrary to public interest: ss 11A(5), 11B; Pt IV Div 3

Review of FOI Decisions

  • Internal review by agency (except decisions of agency head or Minister): s 54
  • Merits review by Information Commissioner (IC review): Pt VII
  • Merits review by AAT at Commonwealth level (Pt VIIA)
  • Appeals to Federal Court on question of law (Pt VII Div 10) or judicial review under ADJR Act

Reasons for Decisions

  • No common law duty to provide reasons, except in 'special' or 'exceptional' circumstances: Public Service Board of NSW v Osmond; Wingfoot Australia Partners Pty Ltd v Kocak
  • Specific statutes often provide a right to reasons
  • Generic right: person with standing under Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) (AAT Act) s 28 and ADJR Act s 13 (unless excluded by Sch 2)
  • Statement of reasons: must include findings on material questions of fact, reference to evidence, and the reasons for the decision: Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (Cth) (AIA) s 25D
  • Under AAT Act s 28 and ADJR Act s 13 (with some exceptions): written statement of findings on material questions of fact must refer to evidence or other material for those findings

Privacy

  • Privacy law regulates intrusions: surveillance, telephone interception, body searches, genetic testing, unauthorised disclosure, handling of personal/health information
  • Most well-known: Privacy Acts
  • Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) regulates how 'APP entities' collect, store, use, correct or disclose 'personal information'
  • APP entity must comply with 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs): s 6, Pts III, IIIA, IIIB
  • 'Personal information' = information or opinion about an identified or reasonably identifiable person: s 6
  • 'APP entity' = government agency, organisation, small business operator (turnover >$3m): ss 6D, 6DA; also health service provider, credit reporting body, business trading in personal information: s 6, Sch 1

Tribunal Review in States & Territories

  • Principal tribunals: civil and administrative tribunals (CATs) with wide jurisdiction
  • Some bodies act as both courts and tribunals (not constrained by SOP): eg NSW Land and Environment Court
  • Unless purporting to exercise federal judicial power: Burns v Corbett

Court Review of Administrative Decisions

Overview

  • JR of an administrative decision is carried out by a court. Unlike MR, JR assesses only the legality of the decision under review
  • Cth decisions reviewed by Federal Court, Federal Circuit and Family Court, or High Court
  • State/territory decisions reviewed by that jurisdiction's Supreme Court
  • JR of subordinate legislation (regulations) is available

Options for JR (Commonwealth)

  • The ADJR Act
  • The constitutionally protected right to JR
  • Specific statutory schemes: eg Migration Act 1958 (Cth) Pt 8; Taxation Administration Act 1973 (Cth) Pt IVC

Interrelated Issues

  • Whether matter is justiciable (can be heard by court)
  • Court's jurisdiction to hear the matter
  • Applicant's standing
  • Applicant's grounds of JR
  • Remedy or remedies sought

The ADJR Act

  • Traditionally the preferred avenue for Cth JR; simplifies complex aspects
  • Can seek review of: (i) a decision of administrative character made under an enactment (ss 3, 5); (ii) conduct for the purpose of making such a decision (s 6); or (iii) failure to make a decision (s 7)
  • 'Decision' = final or operative decision; 'conduct' = acts preparatory to a decision: Bond
  • 'Administrative character' = not legislative or judicial: Federal Airports Corporation v Aerolineas Argentinas
  • 'Made under an enactment' = purported exercise of statutory power (not prerogative/executive): Tang
  • Some decisions expressly excluded: see Sch 1

Standing (ADJR)

  • Applicant must be 'aggrieved' by decision/conduct: ss 5, 6
  • Test largely indistinguishable from general common law 'special interest' test: s 3(4)

Grounds of JR (ADJR Act ss 5 and 6)

  • Largely codify common law JR grounds
  • Exception: 'no evidence' ground extends common law
  • Flexibility via 'abuse of power' and 'otherwise contrary to law'

Remedies (ADJR)

  • Court must maintain distinction between MR and JR
  • Rules simpler than common law: ADJR Act s 16
  • Similar outcomes to common law: Wattmaster Alco v Button
  • Court can: quash/set aside decision; refer matter back to DM with court's directions; declare rights of parties; direct parties to do or refrain from doing; do justice between the parties: Wattmaster; Jadwan; Stambe

Constitutionally Protected JR (Cth)

  • Applicant can seek JR under Australian Constitution, Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) and common law
  • Choice where ADJR Act does not apply, or as alternative
  • Constitution gives HCA original jurisdiction where: Cth is a party (s 75(iii)); person suing on behalf of Cth is a party; writ of mandamus or prohibition sought against Cth officer: s 75(v)
  • Practical operation: applicant can seek JR in FCA in respect of a 'matter' under Judiciary Act 1903 (Cth) (JA) s 39B(1)
  • HCA can remit matters to FCA or state/territory court: JA s 44(2A)

Identifying Error of Law

  • JR proceedings ordinarily resolve questions of law only: Wattmaster; Stambe
  • Where DM fails to make a decision, court can order it be made or similar: s 16(3)
  • Court retains discretion not to grant remedy if another body can deal adequately: s 10(2)(b)(ii)
  • Error of law reviewable whether or not apparent on face of record: ADJR Act ss 5(1)(f), 6(1)(f)

Specific Grounds of JR

Breach of Natural Justice

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(1)(a), 6(1)(a); and constitutionally protected JR
  • Natural justice ('procedural fairness') imposes: the hearing rule and the bias rule
  • Breach = jurisdictional error if there was a possibility of different outcome: Plaintiff S157; SZMTA; MZAPC
  • Unless clearly excluded by legislation or emergency circumstances: Kioa; Miah

The Hearing Rule

  • Applies to relevant decision-making process
  • Applicant should have been given hearing because adverse information is 'credible, relevant and significant': Kioa; Miah; VEAL
  • Content: (i) prior notice; (ii) disclosure of outline/summary of information; (iii) opportunity to comment and respond: Aala

The Bias Rule

  • Applies to relevant decision-making process (almost always)
  • Actual bias (subjective, DM's mind closed: Jia) OR apprehended bias (objective, fair-minded lay observer: Ebner)
  • Categories: DM's interest (financial); DM's conduct (in proceedings); DM's association (with a party); prejudgment; personal prejudice
  • Exceptions: necessity (eg domestic tribunals: British American Tobacco v BATAS); waiver (BATAS); special circumstances (Livesey)

Breach of Statutory Requirement

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(1)(d), 6(1)(d)
  • Where DM breaches a clear, substantive statutory obligatory requirement, decision is unlawful
  • Court asks 'whether it was a purpose of the legislation that an act done in breach should be invalid': Project Blue Sky

Fettering Discretion Through Policy

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(f), 6(2)(f)
  • Government policy must not restrict DM from applying criteria under legislation: Green v Daniels; Prygodicz v Cth (No 2) ('Robodebt')
  • Policy should not be applied inflexibly without regard to merits: MILGEA v Gray; British Oxygen

Dictation

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(e), 6(2)(e)
  • DM entrusted with discretion must exercise it independently, not submit to instructions of another: Rendell; Plaintiff M64/2015; Loielo v Giles

Unauthorised Purpose

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(c), 6(2)(d)
  • DM can only exercise power for the purpose it was conferred. Otherwise unlawful: R v Toohey; Cunneen; Smethurst
  • Two-stage analysis: (i) discern purpose for which power may be exercised; (ii) discern DM's actual purpose: Municipal Council of Sydney v Campbell

Irrelevant Considerations

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(a), 6(2)(a)
  • Decision invalid if DM took into account irrelevant consideration: Trebilco; Murpheyores; Plaintiff M70/2011
  • 'Irrelevant consideration' = one DM is not permitted to consider under the power: obligatory vs permissive considerations

Failure to Take Into Account Relevant Considerations

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(b), 6(2)(b)
  • Decision invalid if DM failed to consider a relevant matter: Peko-Wallsend
  • 'Relevant consideration' = expressly required by statute or implied by court: Yusuf; SZJSS; Carrascalao

'Wednesbury' Unreasonableness

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(2)(g), 6(2)(g)
  • Decision invalid if outcome appears arbitrary, capricious or lacks common sense (Li; Singh) and reasoning 'lacks an evident and intelligible justification': Wednesbury; Li; Stretton; SZMDS
  • Ground used sparingly by courts: SZVFW
  • Objective test: court cannot substitute its own view; asks whether DM acted reasonably: Applicant S20/2002; SZMDS

No Evidence

  • Under ADJR Act ss 5(1)(h), 5(3), 6(1)(h), 6(3)
  • At common law: lack or absence of evidence to satisfy essential statutory element = error of law
  • However: will not be error if evidence is merely inadequate: Melbourne Stevedoring
  • Where statutory criterion is 'state of satisfaction' of DM: reviewable for irrationality if no rational or logical DM could reach on same evidence: SZMDS; Li; FTZK

Limitations on JR

Privative Clauses

  • Statutory provision purporting to limit or preclude JR: Houssein; Bodruddaza; Graham
  • Courts interpret narrowly to preserve JR protected by Constitution s 75: Plaintiff S157; Kirk
  • Minimum Hickman principle: privative clause cannot prevent JR if decision on its face exceeds authority, involves bad faith, or does not relate to subject matter of legislation
  • Court will only conclude privative clause prevents JR if legislation as a whole intended to inhibit JR of that particular form of error: Plaintiff S157

Time Limitation Clauses

  • Statutory provision limiting the period for seeking JR
  • Do not necessarily infringe Constitution s 75 if they simply regulate access
  • But if so restrictive as to effectively prohibit JR: unconstitutional: Bodruddaza

JR of Executive Including Prerogative Powers

  • Courts may review powers not made under statute (prerogative and executive powers of government)
  • Executive power can include powers incidental to statutory functions, as well as prerogative powers (international agreements, royal pardon, honours): Smethwick Corporation; Minister for Arts v Peko-Wallsend Ltd; Vadarlis; Jarratt; CPCF; Ogawa
  • Limitations: executive power may not be used for coercive or intrusive purposes (Vadarlis; Habib) and funding not supported by legislation: Williams (No 1) and (No 2)
  • Constitution s 61 grants Cth 'extends to the execution and maintenance of the Constitution and of the laws of the Commonwealth': Plaintiff S10/2011
  • ADJR Act cannot be used for executive power challenges; use common law in Cth and states under limited grounds: CCSU; Miller (No 2); Plaintiff S10/2011; Davis
  • Executive (including prerogative) power may always be abrogated by statute: De Keyzer's Royal Hotel Ltd
