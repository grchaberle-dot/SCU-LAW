The Core Philosophy

One Brutal Truth

Uni isn't there to make you an expert. It's there to get you a certificate. Your notes exist to help you pass assessments and exams, not to be a comprehensive record of everything you've learned. Every action you take should serve that goal.

Notes = pass tool, not learning journal. Every entry must serve the goal of passing.

5 Key Mindset Shifts

  1. Notes = pass tool, not learning journal
  2. Lectures = reinforcement, not first exposure
  3. Readings = targeted hunting, not passive reading
  4. Tutorials = marker intelligence gathering, not just Q&A
  5. Preparation kills anxiety \u2014 anxiety comes from not knowing or not doing
1. Semester Setup (Do This ONCE, Day One)

Day One Actions

  • Grab the learning guide the moment LMS access opens. It has assessment dates, exam dates, and weekly structure.
  • Calendar everything. Plot all dates and immediately flag conflicts with work.
  • Request extensions EARLY when conflicts arise. Unis are sympathetic to proactive communication; suspicious of last-minute requests.
  • Disclose neurodiversity to coordinators if comfortable. Unlocks accommodations (noise-cancelling headphones, extra time, flexibility). Early = easy; late = suspicious.
2. Master Notes Document (One Per Unit)

Structure

  • Big heading = Unit name
  • Chapters = Weekly themes (e.g., Chapter 1: Offer, Chapter 2: Acceptance)
  • Subheadings within chapters = derived from lecture slides' contents page OR from themes you identify
  • LMS weekly goals copy-pasted in as your compass/guide for each week
This skeleton is your hunting ground. Everything you read, hear, or find gets slotted under these headings.
3. Pre-Lecture Preparation (THE Game-Changer)

Never Go In Cold

Eddie called this the single most powerful thing he's taught students.

  • Read the abridged/student companion text (LexisNexis Butterworths pocket guides) for that week's topic BEFORE the lecture
  • These condense an entire topic (e.g., "Offer" in Contracts) into ~6 small pages of plain language
  • When you walk into the lecture having already read simplified material, the experience completely changes: you're reinforcing, not learning from scratch
  • This also insulates you from bad lecturers (they exist, and you can't control which you get)
  • As you read the abridged text, slot anything relevant into your pre-made note headings

Abridged Resources

  • LexisNexis Butterworths student companions / exam companions
  • Condense full units (e.g., Contracts) into ~100 pages total
  • Older editions (even 10 years old) still useful for stable subjects: Contracts, Torts, Constitutional Law
  • Classic cases (Carbolic Smoke Ball, Donoghue v Stevenson) haven't changed
4. During the Lecture

Structured Listening

  • Take your pre-structured notes INTO the lecture. Don't freehand.
  • Listen for content that fits your pre-made headings and slot it in
  • Don't miss lectures. Lecturers drop assessment hints, exam clues, and tips you won't get anywhere else

4 Universal Legal Listening Keywords

  1. TESTS \u2014 What test applies? (e.g., beyond reasonable doubt, recklessness)
  2. SCOPE \u2014 Subjective vs objective test?
  3. STEPS \u2014 What are the elements/steps to apply the test?
  4. DEFENCES \u2014 What defences exist? (criminal, torts, defamation, property, PI)
These 4 keywords apply across ALL law units. Always be listening for them.
5. Tutorials \u2014 Strategic Use

Value Assessment

  • Only as good as the person running them. If it's the lecturer, prioritise that tute.
  • If marked for attendance, go (free marks)
  • Primary value: asking questions + getting assessment guidance
  • Critical action: Find out WHO is marking your assessment (usually your tutor)
  • Ask them directly: "What do you want to see?" Review the rubric early.
  • Marking varies wildly between markers even after moderation meetings
  • If the tute is unmarked, unhelpful, and not run by the lecturer \u2014 don't feel guilty dropping it after extracting marker intelligence
6. Weekly Readings \u2014 Hunt Mode

You're Not Reading. You're Hunting.

  • Lecturers set unrealistic reading loads (60+ pages per unit per week across multiple units). They don't talk to each other about workload.
  • Read with your note headings in mind. Extract what fits, ignore the rest.
  • For cases: you don't need the full story. You need the rule (the case law principle). Cases always deal with multiple issues; you only need the one relevant to your assessment.
  • Shortcuts: Use case summary websites/textbooks first. Read the full case after if time allows.
Don't pan the entire river. Use a metal detector. Know your target before you start reading.
7. AI-Assisted Research Workflow

What AI Is Good For

  • Summarise cases (3-4 page summaries with admin history, parties, judges, facts, issues, holdings, ratios)
  • Locate specific paragraphs by number in a case
  • Feedback on AGLC referencing and assignment checking
  • Synthesise journal articles and case studies (especially for criminology)

Rules for AI Use

  • Always verify AI output against primary sources \u2014 AI hallucinates case law
  • Never use AI to write your actual assignments. Research, synthesis, verification = fine. Writing = trouble.
  • Everyone is using AI. Not using it means falling behind your cohort.

Study Groups

"If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go in a group."

  • Eddie's results jumped to distinctions (70%+) after forming a group of 4 in his second-last year
  • Benefits: bounce ideas, share resources, discover obscure legislation/procedures, cross-check understanding
  • Even experienced practitioners still discover law they didn't know existed
