Introduction to Legal Systems & Methods (UNE)

1 Foundations of Australian Law

Australia is a common law country with two sources of law: case law (judge-made) and legislation (Parliament-made). The legal system was inherited from England after 1788 colonisation under terra nullius. The Curia Regis evolved into the common law courts, and equity developed through the Court of Chancery. The Australian Constitution (1901) created a federation with divided powers: specific Cth powers in s 51, residual state powers in s 108, and Cth supremacy via s 109. Separation of powers divides government into legislature, executive, and judiciary. The rule of law means everyone is subject to law equally. The Australia Acts 1986 ended remaining UK legal ties. Australia has no constitutionally entrenched Bill of Rights but relies on express/implied constitutional rights and interpretive presumptions.

2 Indigenous Australians & Law

At colonisation Australia was treated as terra nullius. Mabo v Queensland [No 2] (1992) overturned this, recognising native title coexists with Crown radical title where traditional connection is maintained. The Native Title Act 1993 created the claims process. Wik (1996) held native title coexists with pastoral leases. The NT Intervention (2007) used territories power to respond to violence/abuse in Indigenous communities. The Constitution s 51(xxvi) race power allows special laws for people of any race. Circle courts include community in sentencing. Courts recognise social disadvantage (Fernando) but cannot use customary law to excuse criminal behaviour (Crimes Act s 166A).

3 Case Law & Precedent

The doctrine of precedent (stare decisis) ensures certainty, equality, efficiency, and appearance of justice (Telstra v Treloar 2000). Only the ratio decidendi is binding; obiter dicta are persuasive only. Courts are bound by higher courts in the same hierarchy. Difficulties arise when ratio is hard to identify or there is no majority. Courts avoid precedent by distinguishing facts, confining ratio, treating statements as obiter, or overruling (plainly wrong / changed society). The IRAC method (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion) provides the framework for legal analysis. The judicial dilemma: following outdated law risks injustice, but changing it risks accusations of usurping Parliament.

4 Australian Courts & Hierarchy

The High Court (7 justices) sits atop all hierarchies; its rationes bind all courts. The HC is not self-bound but will overrule only where earlier decisions lacked careful principle or led to inconvenience (John/Imbree factors). Full Federal Court requires "plainly wrong" before departing. Farah Constructions (2007) requires intermediate appellate courts to follow other states on Cth/uniform legislation unless plainly wrong. Foreign decisions are persuasive only. Equally divided HC affirms decision below (Judiciary Act s 23(2)(a)). Tribunals don't carry formal precedential value but follow higher tribunal decisions informally.

5 Judicial Decision-Making

Courts determine facts, identify law, and apply law to facts. Questions of fact (jury/judge) use standards of proof: beyond reasonable doubt (criminal) or balance of probabilities (civil). Questions of law are decided by judges and form the basis of appeals. In novel cases courts consider legal authority, principle, and policy. Cattanach v Melchior (2003): 4:3 awarded child-rearing damages applying negligence principles. Appeal courts won't reverse fact findings unless "glaringly improbable" (Fox v Percy). The adversarial system means judges rely on parties to present evidence.

6 Legislation

Legislation is the most important source of law: primary (Acts) and secondary (delegated: regulations, rules, by-laws). Statutes take precedence over delegated legislation and inconsistent case law. A Bill becomes law through proposal, cabinet approval, drafting, passage (3 readings), and Royal Assent. Commencement: 28 days after assent unless specified (AIA s 3A). Structure: short title, definitions (closed="means"; open="includes"), sections, parts, schedules. Repeal can be express or implied; courts resist implied repeal. Sunset clauses cause automatic expiry. Interpretation Acts contain common definitions.

7 Statutory Interpretation I

Goal: give effect to Parliament's intention expressed in the statute. Literal approach: plain meaning even if inconvenient (Engineers' Case). Golden rule: depart from literal to avoid absurdity (Grey v Pearson). Purposive/mischief: Heydon's Case (1584) four questions. Section 15AA AIA requires interpretation that best achieves the Act's purpose. The modern approach (CIC Insurance 1997) insists context from the start, in widest sense including mischief. But s 15AA cannot rewrite legislation or override clear text. Courts must not project own policy views.

8 Statutory Interpretation II

Contextual tools: noscitur a sociis (meaning from accompanying words), ejusdem generis (general limited by specific of same kind), expressio unius (mention of one = exclusion of another). All words carry meaning; consistent use assumed; current meaning applies. Extrinsic materials (second reading speeches, reports, treaties) form context. Under s 15AB AIA: may confirm ordinary meaning OR determine meaning when ambiguous/absurd (threshold test). Common law (CIC Insurance) has no threshold. Key limit: Minister's words don't substitute for text (Re Bolton 1987). s 15AB is permissive not obligatory.

9 Presumptions

Presumptions are common law assumptions about Parliament's purpose, grounded in liberal democratic values, functioning as a "common law bill of rights." All rebuttable by clear words or necessary implication. Key presumptions: no retrospective operation; no interference with fundamental rights (Coco 1994); no abrogation of self-incrimination or legal professional privilege; no deprivation of court access (privative clauses strictly construed); property not taken without compensation; no extraterritorial effect; conformity with international law. Stronger presumption = harder to displace.

10 Obligations & Discretions

"May" = discretion; "shall"/"must" = obligation (AIA s 33; Interpretation Act s 9). But courts look beyond words to purpose/context. Re M (1924): "may" held obligatory because purpose required accuracy. Samad (2002): "may suspend" held discretionary. Project Blue Sky (1998) on breach and invalidity: (A) regulates existing power or states preliminaries? (B) rule-like quality? (C) invalidity causes significant inconvenience? Breach doesn't necessarily produce invalidity; ask Parliament's purpose.

11 Revision & Exam

Typical exam: open book, 3 compulsory questions (5, 10, 25 marks). Know more than basic rules: understand problems in applying them. 11-step statutory interpretation checklist. IRAC for problem questions. Modern/purposive approach preferred (s 15AA). Key takeaway: judicial decision-making = determining facts + identifying law + applying within constraints of precedent, procedure, evidence, and constitutional limits, balancing certainty with justice.
