The Core Shift: Task Controls Structure

Traditional Essay vs Legal Assignment

  • Traditional: Intro, body, conclusion. Goal = summarise what you know about a topic.
  • Legal: Structure follows the task objective. Goal = do a specific job with the material.
  • A letter of advice looks NOTHING like a critical analysis. The format changes to fit what you're asked to do.
In legal writing, the format changes to fit the task, not the other way around.
The Five Legal Writing Styles

1. Advice / Letter of Advice

  • Goal: Apply law to facts. Give practical legal guidance.
  • Start with a scope paragraph: "This advice addresses the following issues..."
  • One heading per issue. Under each: relevant law, application, missing facts, likely outcome.
  • Never give black-and-white answers. Present options A, B, C where facts are uncertain.
  • Lecturers usually provide format guidance or templates.

2. Discussion

  • Goal: Explain a topic and examine it from different perspectives.
  • Demonstrate depth by linking to related principles, overlaps, tensions.
  • Headings and subheadings are essential for separating angles.
  • Introduction = contents page (write it last). Conclusion often not needed.
  • You don't need to judge right/wrong, just show thoughtful engagement.

3. Explanation

  • Goal: Show how a doctrine, process, or mechanism works.
  • Goes deeper than discussion into the mechanics.
  • Often paired with discussion: "Explain X and discuss its effectiveness."
  • Break into stages/elements/moving parts in logical order.

4. Critical Analysis

  • Goal: Evaluate effectiveness, challenge assumptions, weigh competing views.
  • Most complicated style. Requires: explain the issue, evaluate against a standard, challenge perspectives.
  • Distinction level: explains and compares perspectives.
  • HD level: finds outside authorities, introduces their position, agrees/disagrees with reasoning.
  • Headings and subheadings are absolutely essential due to topic breadth.
Critical analysis = evaluation. Not "pick apart" or "find holes." Evaluate effectiveness against the stated objective.

5. Reflection

  • Goal: Examine and articulate a change in your own thinking.
  • NOT: reporting a new fact learned, emotional reaction, or surprise.
  • IS: what you thought before, what challenged that, how thinking changed, why that matters.
  • Weak attempt: "I didn't know X. That was shocking."
  • HD attempt: "I believed only prison was appropriate. Reading [source] challenged that because [reasoning]. Now I think [new position] because [evidence]."
If all you're doing is regurgitating information you learned, you're not reflecting. A reflection is an examination of yourself and your thinking.
Two-Step Universal Planning Process

Step 1: Break Down the Instructions

  • Highlight the task verb (advise, discuss, explain, critically analyse, reflect)
  • Circle the scope words (topic, time period, legislation, parties, objective)
  • List what must be included and what can be excluded
  • Check the rubric for weighting and HD descriptors
  • If the question is vague, the rubric fills the gaps

Step 2: Build the Skeleton First

  • Create headings and subheadings BEFORE doing any research
  • Under each heading, write one sentence about what belongs there
  • Leave intro and conclusion until last
  • Research THEN becomes: "What source do I need for this heading?"
Don't start with research. Planning first prevents confirmation bias, wasted time, and prompt drift. You won't go fishing for material to support a conclusion you've already decided on.

Using the Rubric When the Prompt is Vague

  • Look at the weighting: Analysis/Application usually carries 40-50% of marks
  • Introduction/Conclusion is often only 10%
  • Writing quality and referencing are low-weighted compared to reasoning
  • Model your answer structure on what the HD descriptors describe
  • If you can't figure out what to do: task verb + scope + rubric = your answer
Why Headings Matter

Three Jobs Headings Do

  • 1. Stop you drifting off-topic
  • 2. Break a hard task into smaller manageable parts
  • 3. Help the marker instantly see you've identified the issues (protects against skim-marking)

Break Up Long Paragraphs

  • No rule says one paragraph must contain one issue
  • Headings contain the issues. Paragraphs can be short.
  • A page-long paragraph about defences is unreadable. Use subheadings to segment it.
You can't control who marks your work. If they're skimming, headings save you from losing marks to laziness.
Handling Incomplete Facts

Every Scenario Is Missing Something

  • Lecturers omit facts intentionally. They want you comfortable with grey.
  • Don't guess the missing truth. Instead show: what facts suggest, what else is possible, what's missing.
  • Give multiple outcomes (A, B, C) based on available facts.
  • Use conditional language: "It is likely that...", "On the available facts...", "If the court accepts..."
You are never obligated to give a black-and-white answer. Grey beats fake certainty.
IRAC: Useful but Variable

What IRAC Means

  • I = Issue (what legal question must be answered?)
  • R = Rule (what law applies?)
  • A = Application (how do the facts interact with that law?)
  • C = Conclusion (what is the likely answer?)

The Problem with IRAC

  • Every university (and even individual lecturers) uses a different version
  • La Trobe vs Melbourne expect different presentations
  • Some lecturers start with Conclusion first. Some want full statutory text in Rule. Others just want section numbers.
  • IRAC is a problem-solving formula, not a finished answer format. You still need to convert it into proper paragraphs and headings.

Eddie's IRAC Guidance

  • If IRAC is optional: Don't use it. Essay-style analysis reads more naturally.
  • If IRAC is required: Get the lecturer's specific preferred version/template before writing.
  • IRAC feels very clinical and jarring. That's normal. But if you have a choice, essay format wins.
IRAC formats vary between lecturers. Never assume a generic template is correct. Ask.
Biggest Fail Risks

80% of Fails: Misreading the Question

  • Confusing parties/facts in a scenario
  • Doing a discussion when the prompt said "critically analyse"
  • Misaligning the task verb with what you actually produce
  • Getting the law slightly wrong is recoverable. Getting the question wrong is not.

Pre-Writing Safety Check

  • Read the prompt twice
  • Circle the task verb
  • Identify all parties
  • Make a fact chart or timeline
  • Mark uncertain or disputed facts
  • Compare your plan back to the question before writing
Your biggest enemy in a law degree is not getting the law wrong. It's answering the wrong question. Prompt comprehension is paramount.
