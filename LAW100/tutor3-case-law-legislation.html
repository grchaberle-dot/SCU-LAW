Finding Legislation: Official Sources

Primary Sources by Jurisdiction

  • NSW: legislation.nsw.gov.au (authoritative, most current)
  • Other states: legislation.vic.gov.au, legislation.qld.gov.au, etc.
  • Commonwealth: www.legislation.gov.au
Always verify against official sites. AustLII and Jade are convenient but may lag behind updates.

Paid vs Free Databases

  • Paid (via uni): LexisNexis, Westlaw (preferred for structured presentation, FirstPoint summaries)
  • Free: AustLII (click-through to sections), Jade (clean layout, case links)
  • Westlaw AI-assisted search available at some unis (Swinburne, UniMelb, Monash)
  • UNE has Westlaw + FirstPoint but no AI search or case summaries
Version Control & Temporal Application

Using the Correct Version

  • Use the version date selector on the NSW legislation site (timeline bar)
  • Apply the version of the Act in force at the time of the relevant conduct
  • Sentencing must reflect penalties available at the time of the offence
  • Example: Victorian suspended sentences (abolished ~10 years ago) still applied to historical offences
  • Bail reforms can change overnight: a person arrested pre-midnight faces different laws next day
The date of conduct determines which version of the law applies, not today's date.
Structure of an Act

Standard Layout (Every Act)

  • 1. Preliminaries: Name, commencement, purpose/objects (helps interpretation)
  • 2. Definitions: Key terms explained. WARNING: may apply ONLY to their Part
  • 3. Parts / Divisions / Subdivisions: Organise content by topic
  • 4. Individual Sections: The actual rules, offences, powers
  • 5. Schedules: Lists/enumerations at the end (e.g. which offences are Sched 1 vs Sched 2)

Definitions Trap

  • The same term can mean different things in different Parts of the same Act
  • Example: "prisoner" in Part 1 (false imprisonment victim) vs Part 2 (person in police custody)
  • Always check which Part's definition section governs the section you're reading

Determinative Words (Biggest Student Pitfall)

  • AND = all elements required
  • OR = any one element sufficient
  • MUST / SHALL = mandatory obligation
  • MAY = discretion/permission
  • UNLESS = exception follows
Don't stop reading after finding elements. Sections may continue with defences or exceptions below.

Cross-References

  • An Act may incorporate definitions from another Act (e.g. Crimes Act using Road Safety Act's definition of "motor vehicle")
  • Also common within the same Act: "See definition in section 3"
  • Identify who bears the onus: different tests can be borne by different parties
Regulations (Subordinate Legislation)

Act vs Regulations

  • Act: Primary law (offences, powers, broad framework)
  • Regulations: Operational detail supporting the Act
  • Examples: Road Safety Act + Road Safety Regulations; Bail Act + Bail Regulations; Evidence Act + Evidence Regulations

Practical Impact

  • Offence of running a red light is in the Act
  • Camera placement/visibility standards are in the Regulations
  • Successful challenge: Regulations require 18m unobstructed vision to camera; actual placement around apex only gave 10m. Charges dismissed.
  • Bail surety handling (cash, cheque, bank transfer, when returned) is in Bail Regulations
99.9% of Acts have associated Regulations. Always check for them.
Case Law: Finding & Reading

Reported vs Unreported

  • Reported: Selected for official law report volumes because they contain a recognised legal principle
  • Unreported: Still available in databases but not published in official volumes
  • Both are valid authority; reported cases carry slightly more weight

Citation Anatomy

  • Party names (Applicant v Respondent) + Year + Volume + Court series abbreviation + Page number
  • Unreported cases use neutral citation: e.g. [2026] NSWSC 142
  • Party names SWAP on appeal (appellant goes first)
  • Example: DPP v Fox (Supreme Ct) became Fox v DPP (Court of Appeal) because Fox was now appealing

Judgment Structure (Consistent Across Courts)

  • 1. Administrative history (how did this get here?)
  • 2. Parties, appearances, citation
  • 3. Background / Facts (brief, don't obsess unless critical)
  • 4. Issues identified (legal vs factual)
  • 5. Submissions from each party
  • 6. Court's analysis (cites legislation + prior cases)
  • 7. Decision (the legal conclusion)
  • 8. Orders (the practical outcome, CAN differ from decision)

Legal vs Factual Issues

  • Factual: "Was the person at the location?" (evidence-based)
  • Legal: "Was it self-defence?" (law-based)
  • Appeals are usually on questions of law, not fact
Ratio Decidendi vs Obiter Dicta

Ratio Decidendi (Binding)

  • The legal principle or reasoning that decides the case
  • Binds lower courts in the same hierarchy
  • Example (DPP v Fox): A charge is valid as long as it contains sufficient critical information, even if some particulars are missing

Obiter Dicta (Persuasive)

  • Judicial observations/commentary that are NOT part of the binding ratio
  • Can be persuasive in future cases, especially from respected courts
  • Includes: commentary on strength/weakness of arguments, hypothetical scenarios
Both are useful. If a ratio doesn't help your case, obiter from the same case might.

Decision vs Orders (They Can Differ!)

  • DPP v Fox: Court agreed police were legally correct (DECISION)
  • But dismissed the case anyway (ORDERS) because after 5.5 years, no utilitarian benefit
  • Always distinguish what the court DECIDED from what it ORDERED
Study Strategy for Cases

Practical Workflow

  • Step 1: Stick to lecturer-provided cases ONLY (don't expand the list)
  • Step 2: Check if Westlaw/FirstPoint has a summary. Check textbook.
  • Step 3: If no summary available, copy full case text (from Jade/AustLII) into AI with structured prompt
  • Step 4: AI extracts: administrative history, parties, facts, legal issues, arguments, views
  • Step 5: Read the AI summary FIRST, then skim the actual case (you'll get through it much faster)

Before Reading Any Case

  • Know WHAT you're looking for before you start
  • Identify the specific legal issue/principle you need
  • Skip to relevant headings rather than reading start to finish
  • Note prior authorities cited in the judgment (useful for building arguments later)
Don't pan the entire river for gold when you can use a metal detector. Know your target.

Legislative History for Interpretation

  • Explanatory Memorandum/Notes: The "instruction manual" for why provisions changed
  • Second Reading Speeches: Substantive parliamentary discussion of the Bill's purpose
  • NSW: Bills tab on legislation.nsw.gov.au for Explanatory Notes; Hansard for Second Reading Speeches
  • Use when interpreting purpose or resolving ambiguity in statutory interpretation
