1. Law as a Social Science

Why This Matters

Law is fundamentally different from natural sciences. You cannot empirically test legal concepts because they are socially constructed ideas that exist only because we collectively agree they should.

  • Laws can change because they're based on social agreement, not physical reality (e.g. same-sex marriage, decriminalisation)
  • Laws differ between countries because different societies agree on different things
  • Legal arguments are persuasive, not provable \u2014 you argue for an interpretation, supported by authority
Law provides structure and stability. It achieves this through accessibility, consistency, and a foundational framework that new laws build upon.
2. Where Australian Law Comes From

The Historical Pathway

  1. British monarchy and Anglo-Saxon legal tradition \u2192 established common law system
  2. Magna Carta (1215) \u2192 even the monarch is subject to law (rule of law)
  3. Colonisation \u2192 British legal system transplanted to Australia
  4. Australian Constitution (1901) \u2192 supreme law of the land at Federation

Australian vs US Constitution

  • Australia: Focuses on governmental mechanics (trade, ports, taxes, revenue, military). No explicit bill of rights. Primarily about distributing power between Cth and states.
  • US: Foregrounds individual rights and limits on government power. Bill of Rights is central. Created in revolution against monarchy.
Australia has NO constitutionally guaranteed human rights (with very few implied exceptions). Rights protection comes from legislation, not the Constitution.
3. The Three Branches of Government

Executive (Chapter II)

  • Composition: Governor-General (monarch's representative) + the elected government
  • Function: Administers and enforces law. Makes policy. Introduces bills to Parliament.
  • Does NOT make law directly \u2014 must go through Parliament for legislation

Legislature (Chapter I)

  • Function: Makes statute law (legislation)
  • Structure: House of Representatives (population-based) + Senate (12 per state, equal voice)
  • Process: 1st Reading \u2192 2nd Reading (debate + second reading speech) \u2192 Committee \u2192 3rd Reading \u2192 Senate \u2192 Royal Assent
  • Process takes MONTHS by design (stability requires slow change)

Judiciary (Chapter III)

  • Function: Interprets legislation, applies it, develops common law to fill gaps, resolves disputes
  • Key difference: When a court establishes a legal principle, it applies IMMEDIATELY from judgment date. No months of debate.
  • Courts do NOT make policy, create legislation, or choose which cases to enforce
When the government "announces a policy," that is NOT law yet. It only becomes law when Parliament passes it as a bill. Don't confuse Executive action with legislative action.
4. Legislation and Case Law: The "Happy Marriage"

The Core Rule

Where Parliament has enacted legislation on a point, legislation is supreme. Where legislation is silent or contains gaps, common law governs until Parliament decides to legislate.

Research Steps

  1. Is there legislation on point? If YES \u2192 legislation is your primary authority
  2. Does the legislation cover your specific issue completely? If NO (gap) \u2192 move to step 3
  3. Is there case law filling the gap? The case law IS the law on that point until Parliament legislates
  4. If neither exists, check secondary sources (academic commentary, law reform reports)

Example: "Recklessness" in Victorian Criminal Law

  • Offence: Recklessly causing injury (Crimes Act)
  • Legislation defines "injury" \u2713 and "assault" \u2713 but NOT "recklessness" \u2717
  • Common law fills the gap: The Queen v Campbell [1997] HCA 29
  • Test: prosecution must prove the offender foresaw the outcome as a probable/likely consequence (subjective test)
  • This common law definition IS the law until Parliament inserts a statutory definition
5. Court Hierarchy and Precedent

The Hierarchy

High Court of Australia (apex, binds ALL)\u2193Court of Appeal / Full Federal Court\u2193Supreme Court / Federal Court\u2193District Court\u2193Local / Magistrates Court

How Precedent Works

  • A court is bound by decisions of courts above it in the same hierarchy
  • High Court decisions bind ALL Australian courts (strongest authority)
  • Your state's appellate courts bind lower courts in that state
  • Another state's courts = persuasive only (not binding)
  • International courts = persuasive only
  • Lower court decisions = not binding, rarely even persuasive
When selecting cases for assignments: High Court > your state's appellate courts > other states' appellate courts > foundational older cases.
6. Separation of Powers

The Doctrine

Each branch has its own distinct function. One branch cannot exercise the powers of another.

  • Chapter I \u2192 Legislative power belongs to Parliament
  • Chapter II \u2192 Executive power belongs to the Crown
  • Chapter III \u2192 Judicial power belongs to the courts

Mutual accountability:

  • Courts can declare legislation unconstitutional (judicial review)
  • Executive cannot make law without Parliament
  • Courts cannot choose which cases to prosecute
  • Executive appoints judges but cannot direct their decisions
If legislation conflicts with the Constitution, the Constitution wins. Only a referendum (s 128) can change the Constitution: majority nationally + majority in 4 of 6 states.
7. Division of Powers (Cth vs States)

Section 51: Commonwealth's Shopping List

The Constitution lists specific areas where the Commonwealth can legislate. Everything NOT listed remains with the states.

  • Cth powers (s 51): Trade/commerce, taxation, defence, external affairs, marriage, divorce, corporations, banking, immigration, postal/telecommunications
  • State residual powers: Criminal law, education, health, roads/transport, land/property, police, most tort law

What Happens When Power Is Exceeded?

  • If Cth legislates outside s 51 \u2192 law is invalid (can be struck down)
  • If state law conflicts with valid Cth law \u2192 s 109: Cth prevails, state law inoperative to extent of inconsistency
8. Research Workflow for Assessments

General Approach

  1. Start with legislation \u2014 is there a statute covering your issue?
  2. Check for gaps \u2014 does the statute define everything?
  3. Find case law \u2014 for gaps, interpretation, application examples
  4. Use second reading speeches \u2014 when statutory interpretation is unclear
  5. Academic commentary \u2014 for analysis, critique, policy arguments

Subject-Specific Guidance

  • Contract Law: Predominantly common law. Heavy case reading. Use case summaries.
  • Torts: Mix of statute + case law. Legislation for breach; cases for interpreting elements.
  • Criminal Law: Primarily statute (Crimes Act) + common law for gaps.
  • Evidence Law: Codified in 2008. Prior case law may assist interpretation.
  • Constitutional Law: The Constitution text + High Court interpretation.

Memory Aids

  • "Happy marriage" = legislation + case law working together
  • Legislation is boss when it covers something; common law fills the silence
  • Separation = who does what (branches stay in their lane)
  • Division = who controls what (Cth vs states' turf)
  • s 51 = Commonwealth's shopping list \u2014 not on the list = state matter
  • Slow vs fast: Parliament changes law in months; a Judge changes law overnight
  • High Court = final boss \u2014 binds everyone
  • "Is there an Act?" = always your first research question
  • s 109 = Commonwealth wins when state law conflicts
