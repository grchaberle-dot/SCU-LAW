📜 Week 1: Setting the Scene

Key Definitions

Victimology: The scientific study of victims and victimizations attributable to violations of human rights (Kirchhoff, 1994)
Crime Victim: A person who has suffered harm (physical, emotional, financial) as a result of criminal activity
Victimisation: The process of being made a victim, or the outcome of criminal behaviour directed at a person
Restorative Justice: A process whereby parties affected by an offence collectively resolve how to deal with the aftermath and its implications for the future

Historical Timeline

Pre-modern era: Victims were central to justice. In Anglo-Saxon England (8th century), Moot courts heard accusations directly from victims or their relatives. Compensation ("bot") paid directly to victims.

Norman Conquest (1066): King Henry I began declaring certain offences as "against the King's peace." Henry II developed common law and the State began bypassing victims for prosecution.

18th Century: Private prosecution still dominated. 80% of cases brought by individuals through Associations for Prosecution of Felons. Thief Takers and Bow Street Runners (Henry Fielding) emerged.

19th Century: Prosecution of Offences Act 1879. Police took over from victims as instigators of prosecutions. Victims reduced to witnesses. Professional bodies represented individual victims symbolically.

By WWI: The symbolic/mythic centrality of the victim was embedded in the criminal justice narrative. Private prosecutions became increasingly rare.

Post-WWII: Academics began focusing on victims. Benjamin Mendelsohn (1940) coined the term "victimology." Hans von Hentig (1948) published "The Criminal and His Victim" (13 victim classes).

1960s-70s: Victims movement emerged alongside civil rights. First victim compensation schemes, rape crisis centres, victim surveys (NCS 1972).

1990s: Critical Victimology (Miers 1990). Challenged stereotypes of "ideal victims" (white, middle-class, male).

21st Century: Indigenous Sentencing Courts (Port Adelaide 1999). Restorative justice programs. Green victimology emerging.

Three Approaches to Victimology

  • Penal Victimology (Von Hentig): focused on victim-offender interaction within criminal law
  • General Victimology (Mendelsohn/Dussich): broader focus on lessening "victimity," all types of victims
  • Human Rights Victimology (Elias/Kirchhoff): victims of abuse of power, state crime, structural violence

Mendelsohn's 6 Victim Types (1956)

  • Completely innocent victim (ideal victim)
  • Victim with minor guilt (ignorance)
  • Victim as guilty as offender (mutual participation)
  • Victim more guilty than offender (provocation)
  • Most guilty victim (aggressor/attacker killed in self-defence)
  • Imaginary victim (false claims)

Key Organisations

World Society of Victimology (WSV): Founded 1979, ECOSOC consulting status, tri-annual symposia. Four aims: research, advocacy, services, UN cooperation.

UN Declaration of Basic Principles of Justice for Victims (1985): Broad definition including victims of abuse of power.

📊 Week 2: Measuring Victimisation

The "Dark Figure" of Crime

The gap between actual crime occurring and crime recorded in official statistics. Many crimes go unreported or undetected. Most unreported: sexual assault, domestic violence, household larceny.

Official Data Sources

UCR (Uniform Crime Reports, 1931): FBI collection from ~18,000 agencies. Part I index offences (8 crimes). Uses hierarchy rule (only most serious offence counted). Limited: misses non-aggravated assault, kidnappings, many others.
NIBRS (National Incident-Based Reporting System): Replaced UCR limitations. 22 crime categories, 53 data elements per incident. Records ALL offences (no hierarchy rule). Captures victim-offender relationship, injuries, weapons, property loss.

Victimisation Surveys

NCVS (National Crime Victimization Survey, 1973): US nationally representative sample, 12+ years old. Households stay 3 years, 7 interviews at 6-month intervals. Captures reasons for non-reporting. Limitations: no under-12s, no homeless, recall bias.
CSEW (Crime Survey for England & Wales): Since 1982 (formerly BCS). Annual since 2000. Cluster-based sampling. Children 10-15 included since 2009.
ICVS (International Crime Victims Survey, 1989): 78 countries, completed 5 times. Cross-national comparison. 3 crime groups: vehicle-related, burglary/breaking & entering, personal crimes.

Australian Data Sources

  • ABS Recorded Crime (Victims) + Causes of Death
  • General Social Survey
  • National Crime & Safety Survey
  • Women's Safety Survey / Personal Safety Survey
  • Administrative data from police, hospitals, community services

Admin Data Decision Chain

Victim recognition → Decision to notify police → Police detection → Police data entry. At each stage, cases can drop out of official statistics.

Key Reading: Baumer & Lauritsen (2010)

Multivariate analysis of police reporting 1973-2005. Found increases in reporting across violence types, influenced by: weapon presence, injury severity, financial loss, victim demographics, community policing, 911 systems, Neighbourhood Watch, Victims of Crime Act 1984.

👤 Week 3: Individual Sources of Victimisation

Demographic Factors

Age: Youth at highest risk (age-crime curve applies to victims too). NCVS data: ages 12-17 have highest victimisation rates. Risk decreases with age after young adulthood.

Gender: Males have higher rates of violent victimisation overall. Females have higher rates for IPV and sexual assault specifically. Gap in offending mirrors gap in victimisation.

Race: Racial minority groups disproportionately victimised. Indigenous Australians: 1 in 4 reported physical/threatened violence in prior 12 months (double the 1994 rate). After age adjustment: Indigenous = double victimisation rate of non-Indigenous. Young men 15-24 highest (38%).

Aboriginal women: 32x more likely hospitalised for family violence
Diverse sexuality/gender: very high prevalence of abuse (Higgins et al., 2024)

Lifestyle Theory (Hindelang, Garofalo & Gottfredson, 1978)

Victimisation risk is shaped by lifestyle patterns: where you go, when, with whom. Demographic characteristics influence role expectations and structural constraints, which shape lifestyle, which determines exposure to risk.

Routine Activity Theory (Cohen & Felson, 1979)

Three-part heuristic: Crime occurs when three elements converge in time and space: (1) a motivated offender, (2) a suitable target, and (3) the absence of a capable guardian.

Originally explained post-WWII crime increases despite rising prosperity: more women working = more empty homes = less guardianship.

Self-Control Theory (Schreck, 1999)

Low self-control linked to victimisation (not just offending). People with low self-control take more risks, associate with offenders, and place themselves in dangerous situations. Pratt, Turanovic, Fox & Wright (2014) meta-analysis of 66 studies confirmed the link.

Early Theorists

Von Hentig (1948): 13 victim classes, intelligence-victimisation link, sociobiological approach. Never empirically supported. Victim-blaming orientation.

Wolfgang (1958): Victim-precipitated homicide. Victims often young males of minority group who started the event. Hyper-individualistic, no social context.

Fattah's 10 Categories (1991)

  • Opportunities for crime
  • Risk factors (demographics)
  • Motivated offenders in area
  • Exposure to potential offenders
  • Associations with criminal others
  • Dangerous times and places
  • Dangerous behaviours
  • High-risk activities
  • Defensive/avoidance behaviours (protective)
  • Structural/cultural proneness

🧠 Week 4: Offender Perspectives & Social Learning

Social Learning Theory (Akers)

Criminal behaviour is LEARNED through social interaction. Four key concepts:

  • Differential Association: Who you associate with matters. Priority, duration, frequency, intensity of contact with criminal vs prosocial others.
  • Definitions: Attitudes/meanings attached to behaviour. Positive definitions of crime increase likelihood of offending.
  • Differential Reinforcement: Balance of rewards vs punishments (actual and anticipated) for behaviour.
  • Imitation: Observing and copying behaviour of others (Bandura's Bobo Doll, 1961).

Sutherland's 9 Factors of Criminal Behaviour

Criminal behaviour is learned in interaction, principally in intimate personal groups. Learning includes techniques + motives/drives/attitudes. Direction of motives learned from definitions of legal code as favourable/unfavourable. Excess of definitions favourable to violation = criminal behaviour.

Strain Theory (Agnew, 2001/2002)

Three categories of strain that can lead to crime:

  • Loss of legal avenues to achieve goals (blocked opportunities)
  • Loss of positively valued stimuli (death of loved one, end of relationship)
  • Experiences of negatively valued stimuli (abuse, bullying, victimisation)

Direct strain (personal experience) vs indirect/vicarious strain (witnessing others' victimisation).

Case Study: Terrence Williams

Born 27 Feb 1968. Raped at age 6, molested by teacher, sexually assaulted by multiple men including Amos Norwood and Herbert Hamilton (his victims), gang-raped in juvenile detention age 18.

Convicted 1984: third-degree murder (Hamilton) + first-degree murder (Norwood) = death sentence.

At trial, NONE of his victimisation was mentioned. Execution warrant signed Aug 2012, stayed by Philadelphia State Court. 150+ former judges, prosecutors, jurors signed petition for commutation. Jurors said they'd have chosen life, not death, if they'd known.

Key lesson: Perfect example of victim-offender overlap and how the CJS fails to recognise the cycle of violence.

Cycle of Violence (Widom, 1989)

Physical abuse in childhood → increased likelihood of adult violent behaviour. But NOT inevitable. Re-analysis showed childhood NEGLECT is actually a stronger predictor of adult violence than physical abuse.

Protective factors: prosocial activities, school attachment, non-deviant peers, self-control.

🏙️ Week 5: Situations & Structural Contexts

Structural Context

Victimisation doesn't happen in a vacuum. Structural constraints (economic, familial, educational) limit behavioural options and shape exposure to risk (Hindelang et al., 1978, p.242).

Social disorganisation: Shaw & McKay (1942). Neighbourhoods with poverty, residential instability, and ethnic heterogeneity have weakened informal social controls → more crime/victimisation.

Critical vs Positivist Victimology

  • Positivist: focuses on individual risk factors, demographics, measurable variables
  • Critical: examines structural inequality, power relations, how marginalised groups are disproportionately victimised

"Code of the Street" (Anderson, 1999)

In disadvantaged urban communities, respect/reputation becomes social currency. Violence used to maintain status. "Street efficacy" (Sharkey, 2006): ability to navigate dangerous environments without victimisation.

Social Exclusion

The most disadvantaged groups experience highest victimisation: homeless (5x sexual assault, 3x household crimes), mentally ill, migrants. Process of demonisation: The Exclusive Society. CJS limitations for marginalised groups.

Risky Routines Revisited

Routine Activity Theory ≠ Lifestyle Theory (different mechanisms). Turanovic & Pratt (2019, p.71): risky lifestyle increases probability of REPEAT victimisation specifically. Stewart, Schreck & Simons (2006): youth street crime ↔ disadvantaged neighbourhoods (reciprocal relationship).

Feminist Perspective

Power over women as structural context for victimisation. Gender-based violence not reducible to individual risk factors. Must examine patriarchal structures and cultural tolerance of violence.

💥 Week 6: Personal, Social & Political Consequences

Personal Consequences

68% of violent crime victims (12+) experience socio-emotional problems. More severe when victimised by someone known (intimate partner > stranger).

  • Physical: injuries, chronic pain, disability, death
  • Psychological: PTSD, depression, anxiety, self-medication
  • Financial: medical costs, lost income, property damage
  • Fight/flight/freeze response: hyperactivation in children → structural brain changes

ACE Study (Adverse Childhood Experiences)

10 categories scored 0-10. Higher ACE score = dramatically increased risk of: substance abuse, depression, suicide attempts, heart disease, cancer, early death. Impacts brain/CNS development. "Trauma tree" metaphor: roots (prenatal/infancy), trunk (childhood), branches (adolescence/adulthood).

Resilience

Not purely individual: also a structural quality. Protective factors: supportive social ties, prosocial activities, self-control, school engagement. Coping: healthy (seeking support, counselling) vs unhealthy (substance use, withdrawal, aggression).

Social Consequences

  • Peers avoid friendships with victimised youth (fear of similar targeting)
  • Adolescent victims form friendships with OTHER victims (elevated mutual risk)
  • Supportive social ties = positive coping (emotional, social, instrumental support)
  • Social ties are age-graded, develop through cumulative continuity

Political Consequences

Fear of crime: Weaponised by politicians. Examples: Willy Horton ad (US 1988), Howard's "A Safe and Stronger Australia," NT Bail Act/Youth Justice Act 2021.

Mandatory sentencing: Disproportionately impacts Indigenous people, mentally ill, and juveniles. Removes judicial discretion.

Bandura's Bobo Doll (1961): Social learning in victimisation contexts. Children imitate aggressive behaviour observed in adults (gender differences in imitation patterns).

⚖️ Week 7: Victims & the Criminal Justice System

Historical Evolution

Eye-for-an-eye → Crown took over fines → Adversarial system marginalised victims → Industrial Revolution made justice less personal → Indigenous restorative justice oppressed under colonialism → Early 1900s victims' movement pushed back.

Today: victim = mere witness under adversarial system. Main duty is providing information during investigative phase.

Secondary Victimisation

Negative interactions with CJS that re-victimise. Half of rape victims report being treated poorly by police. Victims forced to testify without being given opportunity to decide whether/how to reveal information.

Victims & Courts

  • Prosecutor discretion: "Downstream orientation" (anticipating what jury will accept)
  • "Genuine victim" stereotype: credible, innocent, undeserving
  • Extra-legal factors: age, lifestyle, moral character influence decisions
  • Only 10% of violent crime victims receive victim-witness services

Victim Impact Statements

Recognised in every US state + UK, Canada, Australia, NZ. Debate: do they bias jurors (Paternoster & Deise, 2011) or are they therapeutic for victims? Research is mixed.

Victim Compensation

State payments funded from offender fines. Narrow class: violent crimes only. Few victims actually receive it. NSW: 2-year time limit with 3 exceptions.

Australian Framework

Victims Rights & Support Act (NSW) 2013: 18 rights in the Charter of Victim Rights including: right to be treated with courtesy/compassion, right to information about investigation progress, right to give VIS, right to compensation.

Issues in the Field

  • Victimology reinforces helplessness/passivity perceptions
  • Maintained distinction between "those who victimise" and "those who are victimised" (ignores overlap)
  • Legal status debate: are victims users of CJS, or violators of the law themselves?
  • Difficulty incorporating victim perspective via VIS while maintaining rights of defendants

🏘️ Week 8: Impact on Communities

Broken Windows (Kelling, 1982)

Physical/social disorder signals that informal controls have broken down → spiral of decline → more crime. Disorder includes: graffiti, abandoned buildings, public intoxication, aggressive panhandling.

Residential Turnover

Victimisation as both CAUSE and CONSEQUENCE of residential instability. Victimised people move away → neighbourhood destabilised → more victimisation for those who stay.

Collective Efficacy

Mutual trust + willingness to intervene for common good. Weakened by victimisation (reciprocal relationship). Shaw & McKay (1942): social disorganisation in transitional zones.

Fear of Crime

Categorical contagion: one crime in an area affects perception for entire community. Policy makers exploit fear for political gain. Fear often disproportionate to actual risk (elderly fear crime most but are victimised least).

🚫 Week 9: Violence Against Women

1 in 3 women experience IPV/sexual violence globally
Rural women: 1.4x more likely to experience IPV
Aboriginal women: 32x more likely hospitalised for family violence

Sexual Assault

Definition evolved from 1970s narrow rape statutes to 2012 FBI gender-neutral redefinition. Rape myths ("she asked for it," "real rape" requires physical force/stranger) impact police and prosecutor decision-making.

Intimate Partner Violence

Pre-1970s treated as "domestic issue." Younger and Black women 2.5x more likely to be victims. Average 7 attempts to leave before permanently leaving. Most dangerous point = when they leave.

Walker's Cycle of Violence (1979)

Tension-building phase → Acute battering incident → Honeymoon/reconciliation phase → Repeat (escalating)

Rural IPV (Australian Context)

Skye Saunders' Whispers from the Bush (107 rural women interviewed). DeKeseredy: rural IPV research gaps in Australia. Alston: under-reporting barriers include police availability, stigma, geographic isolation, economic dependence.

Our Watch: Intersectional approach needed (gender + race + location + socioeconomic status).

Police Responses

Arrest as deterrent (Minneapolis Domestic Violence Experiment). Mandatory reporting policies. Pro-arrest policies. Debate over whether arrest actually reduces recidivism.

🔒 Week 10: Victimisation in Prisons

Nature & Extent

Interpersonal conflicts, group violence, sexual victimisation. Men = more physical violence, women = 2x sexual victimisation risk. Younger inmates more vulnerable. Minorities overrepresented.

Contextual Factors

  • "Unstructured routines" (idle time)
  • Adjustment problems (new inmates)
  • Rule-breaking behaviour → elevated risk
  • Security level, overcrowding (complex relationship)

Theoretical Frameworks

Deprivation Model (Sykes, 1958): "Pains of imprisonment" create conditions for victimisation. Argot roles: rats, gorillas, wolves, ball busters, real men. Prison culture develops IN RESPONSE to deprivation.
Importation Model: Pre-prison lives imported in. Criminal history, gang membership, substance abuse carried into prison environment.
DiIulio (1987): Prison MANAGEMENT is the key variable. Well-run prisons have less violence regardless of population characteristics.

Consequences

Harsh conditions → higher recidivism. "Victimization effect": even WITNESSING violence in prison causes trauma. Failed community re-entry. NT Juvenile Detention Royal Commission (2016) exposed systemic abuse.

💻 Week 11: Cybervictimisation

Key Concepts

"Genius myth" debunked: Most cybercrime is mundane/low-level, not sophisticated hacking. Age-crime curve applies online too (young people = more victims despite tech literacy). Older people disproportionately hit by financial fraud.

Applying RAT to Cybercrime

Yar (2005): Challenged applicability. "Spatiotemporal ontology" issue: online space doesn't have the same convergence of offender/target/guardian in physical space. Called it "old wine in new bottles" (p.424).

Leukfeldt & Yar (2016): Concluded RAT isn't fully feasible for cyber due to methodological limitations.

Reyns (2013): Extended RAT to crimes without physical proximity (identity theft, 5,985 respondents). Found online routine activities DO predict victimisation.

Guardianship Paradox

Marcum (2009) & Reyns (2010, p.156): Protective software actually increased or had no effect on victimisation likelihood in some cases. Possible explanation: false sense of security → riskier online behaviour.

COVID-19 & Cybercrime

Hawdon, Parti & Dearden (2020): Lockdown reduced street crime but did NOT increase cybercrime victimisation (counterintuitive). Despite more time online, routine activities shifted in ways that didn't necessarily increase suitable targets.

Cyberbullying

Definition: unwanted aggressive behaviour, power imbalance, repeated over time. Self-control + social learning theory both apply to online deviance. Online bullying most common form among youth.

Identity Theft

Williams (2016): Correlation between participant's online routine activities (downloading, shopping, banking) and perceived risk of identity theft victimisation. British Crime Survey data. Limited by non-specific survey question wording.
