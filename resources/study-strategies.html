1. Weekly Study System

Priority order each week

  1. Read tutorial problem questions first before any readings. This filters what matters.
  2. Skim lecture slides for relevant law (sections, cases, principles).
  3. Skim textbook only for sections needed to answer tutorial questions.
  4. Build a "summary of law" template that can answer those tutorial problems.
  5. Attend tutorial, update template with anything discussed in class.
  6. Generate 2 practice problems (use AI) and attempt them over the following weeks.

Time management

  • Schedule the same study time for each subject weekly (minimum 1hr, ideally 3hrs).
  • Use 50 minutes on / 10 minutes off cycles. Longer break (30 min) after two cycles.
  • Never miss two study days in a row for the same subject.
  • During breaks: move, breathe fresh air, no screens.
Key principle: Design all study to produce assessment-ready notes. If your notes can't answer a tutorial problem, they're incomplete.

2. Building Law Summary Templates

The element-based method

Every law (statutory or common law) can be broken into elements: the key words or phrases that each require their own definition or legal test.

  1. Start with the source. If codified: quote the section. If common law: cite the originating or current leading case.
  2. Identify burden of proof if relevant (prosecution/plaintiff).
  3. Break into elements. Each ambiguous word or phrase becomes a subheading.
  4. Define each element using (in order): the Act's own definitions → regulations → Interpretation Act → case law.
  5. Add case examples. 2 cases where the element was satisfied, 2 where it was not. Summarise facts + outcome in 2 sentences each.

Example structure

Offence: [Section] of [Act]

Element 1: "[word]" → defined in s X as...

Element 2: "[word]" → test from [Case]: ...

Application cases: Satisfied in [Case A]; Not satisfied in [Case B]

Why this works: In exams you copy your template, then only need to focus on applying the law to the facts. No freezing, no flipping through pages.

3. Answering Problem Questions

How lecturers write questions

  • They pick 2–4 weeks of content, write an answer first, then create facts that produce that answer, then scramble the facts so they appear in random order.
  • Some facts are easy (clear answer), some are debatable (both sides needed).
  • Almost every sentence of facts is relevant. If you haven't used a fact, reconsider.

The 7-step process

  1. Identify all legal issues. Write a heading for each.
  2. Group relevant facts under each heading.
  3. State the relevant law (copy from your template).
  4. Apply law to facts. Each sentence of application = law + facts combined.
  5. Argue both sides where the answer is debatable.
  6. Conclude on each issue (clear answer, one side stronger, or genuinely unclear).
  7. Re-read the question. Check every sentence of facts is addressed.

Application technique

Every application sentence should combine a legal principle with a specific fact:

"Refraining from doing what one is otherwise able to do lawfully can constitute consideration (Dunton v Dunton), and so Sally's promise to stop drinking satisfies this requirement."

Marks split: Roughly 50% for accurate law, 50% for identifying all relevant arguments and reaching reasonable conclusions.

4. Writing Legal Essays

The research-to-writing pipeline

  1. Research broadly using AI deep research or legal databases. Collect 10–15 sources.
  2. Summarise each source with direct quotations + page numbers in a single "unorganised research" document.
  3. Identify themes. What arguments appear in multiple sources? Where do authors agree or disagree?
  4. Develop a thesis. Central argument + 2–4 supporting reasons. Not an opinion: a conclusion drawn from the research.
  5. Organise research by argument. New document with headings for each argument; copy relevant passages under each.
  6. Plan structure: Introduction (thesis) → Background law + counter-arguments → Argument 1 → Argument 2 → Argument 3 → Conclusion.
  7. Write the body first, introduction and conclusion last.
  8. Proofread after every paragraph, after every session, and once more 2 days later.

Thesis structure

A thesis contains: (a) an overall argument, and (b) 2–4 reasons why that argument is correct, which become your paper's sections.

Writing tips

  • Short sentences beat long ones. If a sentence needs a semicolon, split it.
  • Never start by summarising the facts. Facts appear only during application.
  • Use headings aggressively (markers skim).
  • Introductions and conclusions have no footnotes (they summarise your own conclusions).
  • Clear writing that anyone could follow beats dense "sophisticated" prose every time.

5. Statutory Interpretation Framework

The only approach that matters: contextual

Australian courts use one approach: the contextual approach. Words are defined so they are consistent with the statute's purpose, considering context from the outset (not only when ambiguity arises).

Key cases: CIC Insurance v Bankstown Football Club (1997) 187 CLR 384; Project Blue Sky v ABA (1998) 194 CLR 355.

Step-by-step process

  1. Identify the relevant section of legislation.
  2. Break it into elements (key words/phrases).
  3. For each element, check definitions: (a) within the same Part, (b) in the Act's definitions section, (c) in regulations, (d) in the jurisdiction's Interpretation Act.
  4. If still ambiguous: check whether courts have defined the word (use Jade Citator or Lexis Advance → Legislation Title + Provision Number).
  5. If no court definition: apply the contextual approach using extrinsic materials.

Extrinsic materials (to find purpose)

  • Second reading speeches (Hansard)
  • Explanatory memoranda
  • Law Reform Commission reports
  • Parliamentary committee reports
  • International conventions

Additional interpretation rules

  • Generalia specialibus: Specific provision overrides a general one.
  • Ejusdem generis: "and other things" limited to the same type as the listed items.
  • Noscitur a sociis: Words in a list take meaning from their neighbours.
  • Criminal provisions: Interpreted narrowly; ambiguity resolved in favour of the accused.

NSW Interpretation Act 1987 s 34

Permits use of extrinsic materials to confirm meaning or resolve ambiguity. Commonwealth equivalent: Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (Cth) s 15AB.

6. Using Legal Databases

Which database for what

  • Jade (jade.io) → Citator: find all cases that have considered a section of legislation. Best case coverage in Australia.
  • Lexis Advance → Advanced Search for cases by Legislation Title + Provision Number. Also: CaseBase summaries, Halsbury's Laws of Australia, journal articles.
  • AGIS Plus Text → Best for Australian law journal articles (tick "full text only").
  • Westlaw → Cases + journals + FirstPoint summaries.
  • HeinOnline → International/comparative law journal articles (US, UK).
  • Halsbury's Laws of Australia → Pre-made summaries of law broken into elements. Great starting scaffold.

Efficient search techniques

  • Search by case citation (e.g. "(1997) 187 CLR 384") not case name.
  • Search for distinctive legal terminology ("doli incapax", "ejusdem generis") to narrow results.
  • For essays: search Legislation Title + Provision Number in Secondary Materials to find journal articles discussing that section.

7. Using AI for Legal Research (Safely)

The golden rule

Never solely rely on anything AI generates. AI locates and summarises. You verify.

The safe workflow

  1. Use AI to locate sources (legislation, cases, journal articles). Use deep research / research mode where available.
  2. Verify every source exists. Find the PDF on the official legislation site, Jade, or Lexis.
  3. Attach the PDF to AI and ask it to summarise with pinpoint references (page/paragraph numbers).
  4. Check the pinpoints. Open the PDF, go to the cited page, confirm the passage exists.
  5. If confirmed: you now have a 100% accurate summary you can cite.

What AI is good for

  • Finding relevant sections of legislation quickly.
  • Locating cases that have considered a section.
  • Summarising attached case PDFs with pinpoint citations.
  • Generating practice problem questions for spaced repetition.
  • Explaining difficult legal concepts in plain language.
  • Checking your summary of law for missing elements or tests.

What AI is NOT good for

  • Writing assessment answers (academic misconduct + usually wrong).
  • Providing case law without verification (hallucinations are common).
  • Replacing your own understanding of the law.
UNE policy: Always check your institution's AI use policy before using AI for assessed work. Using AI for research (locating + verifying sources) is generally permitted; using AI to generate answers is not.

8. Retention & Exam Prep

Spaced repetition for law

  1. Week of tutorial: Build your template + answer the tutorial problem.
  2. 1 week later: Attempt an AI-generated problem on the same topic without notes first.
  3. 2 weeks later: Attempt a second AI-generated problem.
  4. Exam prep: Attempt past exam questions using your template.

By exam time you've answered 4+ problems on every topic and your template is battle-tested.

Exam day strategy

  • Read the whole question twice. Write legal issues in the margin as you spot them.
  • Do confident issues first (fast, high marks). Save hard ones for remaining time.
  • Copy your law template directly into your answer. Focus brain power on application.
  • Never begin by restating the facts. Facts only appear during application.

The anti-overwhelm rescue plan

  1. Write the task verb (advise, discuss, explain, critically analyse, reflect).
  2. Write the scope words (topic, parties, legislation, timeframe).
  3. Copy rubric criteria into plain English.
  4. Create headings only.
  5. Put one sentence under each heading saying what belongs there.
  6. Start research/writing only after that skeleton exists.
