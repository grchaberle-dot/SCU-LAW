1. The Big Picture

Here's the truth about law school that nobody tells you upfront: you cannot learn everything. There's too much content every week. The trick is knowing what actually matters for your assessments and building systems around THAT.

This system has one goal: when you sit down for an exam or submit an assignment, you already have everything you need because you built it during the trimester.

The Core Philosophy

  • AI helps you learn, practice, and remember but never does the thinking for you
  • Templates save you in exams because you built them weeks ago and practiced with them
  • Spaced repetition means you remember because you revisit things at the right intervals
  • Everything is assessment-focused from Week 1 so nothing is wasted effort
  • You use two sets of notes: a big detailed dump (for learning) and condensed templates (for exams)
💡 The LEGO Analogy: Studying law is like building a complex LEGO set. If you tip all the pieces on the floor and hope they fit together, you get chaos. But with step-by-step instructions (your templates), even the most complicated design comes together smoothly. Your templates ARE your instructions.

What Makes This Different

Most students read passively, highlight stuff, and then freeze in exams because they never practiced APPLYING the law. This system is different because:

  1. You read the tutorial questions FIRST (so you know what matters)
  2. You build templates that can answer ANY question on that topic
  3. You practice answering problems using your templates (spaced out over weeks)
  4. By exam time, you just copy your template and focus on the application

2. Your Weekly Rhythm

Consistency beats intensity. You need a routine where you study the same subjects at the same time each week. Here's what a solid week looks like for 3 units:

Before the Week Starts

  • Look at your calendar/ClickUp for the week
  • Check what tutorials are coming up
  • Check if any assessment due dates are within 2 weeks

The Weekly Study Cycle (Per Subject)

Step 1: Read Tutorial Questions First (15 mins)

Before you touch the textbook or lecture, read this week's tutorial/seminar questions. This tells you exactly what content matters. If a section of the textbook doesn't help answer a tutorial question, you can safely skip it.

Step 2: Watch Lecture / Skim Readings (60-90 mins)

Now watch/skim with PURPOSE. You're looking for the law needed to answer those tutorial questions. Take notes on: key sections of legislation, key cases, key principles. Don't write everything down, just what helps answer the tutorial Qs.

Step 3: Build/Update Your Template (45-60 mins)

This is where the magic happens. Take what you learned and build a step-by-step template for answering questions on this week's topic. (See Section 4 for exactly how to do this.)

Step 4: Answer the Tutorial Problem (30-45 mins)

Use your template to answer the tutorial question. This is practice for your exam/assignment.

Step 5: Attend Tutorial & Update (during class + 15 mins after)

Compare your answer to what's discussed. Update your template with anything you missed.

Step 6: Generate Practice Questions (10 mins)

Ask AI to generate 2-3 similar practice questions (with answers you DON'T read yet). Save them for your spaced practice sessions.

The Golden Rules

  • Never miss two days in a row. One missed day is fine. Two starts forming a new habit of not studying.
  • Use Pomodoro (25 min work / 5 min break) or the 50/10 rule (50 min work / 10 min break). After 2-4 cycles, take a proper 20-30 min break.
  • Step away from screens during breaks. Walk, stretch, breathe outside. Your brain consolidates info during rest.
  • Minimum 3 hours per subject per week. More during assessment crunch.
🎯 Where Your Time Goes: About 80% of your study time should go into building and practicing with templates. The other 20% is reading/watching lectures. Templates ARE the work.

3. How to Read & Take Notes

Passive reading (just reading without doing anything) is basically useless for law. Your brain doesn't retain information that way. Here's how to read actively:

The Active Reading Method

  1. Read tutorial questions first so you know what you're hunting for
  2. Skim the textbook chapter looking ONLY for content relevant to those questions
  3. When you find something relevant, stop and summarise it in your own words
  4. If a section isn't relevant to a tutorial question, skip it. Seriously. You can't read everything.

Aligning Reading with Assessments

  • Weeks 1-5ish: Focus on content likely in your mid-trimester assessment
  • Weeks 6-12ish: Focus on content likely in your final exam
  • Always check: Does the assignment question tell me which weeks are being assessed?

What to Actually Write Down

When you find relevant content, you're extracting it for your template. You want:

  • The exact section number of any legislation mentioned
  • The name and citation of key cases
  • What the case decided (the ratio, in one sentence)
  • Any legal test or definition the court created
  • Key quotes from judges (with paragraph numbers for AGLC referencing)
⚠️ Don't fall into these traps:
• Highlighting without summarising = waste of time
• Reading every page of a chapter = impossible to retain
• Rewriting the textbook = you're not learning, you're copying
• Reading without connecting to assessment = unfocused effort

4. Building Your Law Templates (The Secret Weapon)

This is the single most important skill in law school. A template is a pre-written summary of the law on a specific topic, structured so you can use it to answer ANY exam question on that topic. You build it during the trimester, and in the exam you just copy it and add your application to the facts.

How to Build a Template (Step by Step)

Step 1: Start with the Source of the Law

If the law is in legislation (a statute/Act), quote the relevant section. If it's common law (made by judges), cite the case that created or most recently stated the principle.

Example: "A person who has a prohibited drug in their possession is guilty of an offence" (Drug Misuse and Trafficking Act 1985 (NSW) s 10).

Step 2: Break It Into Elements

Every legal rule has components (elements) that must each be proven. Identify them and give each its own subheading.

Example: Section 10 has two elements: (1) "prohibited drug" and (2) "possession".

Step 3: Define Each Element

For each element, find how it's defined. Check in this order:

  1. Is it defined in the same chapter of the Act?
  2. Is it defined in the definitions section of the Act?
  3. Is it defined in the regulations?
  4. Is it defined in the Interpretation Act for that jurisdiction?
  5. Has a judge defined it in a case? (Check Jade or Lexis)

Step 4: Add the Legal Tests

Some elements have multi-part tests that courts have developed. Include these with the case citation.

Example: "Possession" requires: (a) physical possession or control (Moors v Burke), AND (b) knowledge that the thing is a prohibited drug (He Kaw Teh v R).

Step 5: Add Case Examples (Both Sides)

Include 2-3 cases where the element WAS satisfied, and 2-3 where it WASN'T. Brief fact summaries only (2-3 sentences each). These give you a spectrum to compare your exam facts against.

What a Finished Template Looks Like

OFFENCE: [Name of offence]

Source: [Quote the section or state the common law principle + case]

Burden of proof: [Who must prove what? Usually prosecution beyond reasonable doubt]

ELEMENT 1: [word/phrase from the section]
Definition: [where it's defined + what it means]
Legal test: [if applicable]
Key cases: [1-2 sentences each]
→ [APPLICATION GOES HERE IN EXAM]

ELEMENT 2: [word/phrase from the section]
Definition: ...
Legal test: ...
Key cases: ...
→ [APPLICATION GOES HERE IN EXAM]

CONCLUSION: [Template sentence: "Therefore, [client] is likely/unlikely to be found guilty of [offence] because..."]
💡 Why this works in exams: You already have the law written perfectly. In the exam, you just copy it and spend ALL your brainpower on the application (matching the facts to the law). No more freezing. No more panicking about what the law is. It's already there.

When to Combine Templates

Sometimes an exam question covers multiple weeks. For example, a negligence question needs: duty of care + breach + causation + defences + damages. Once you have individual templates for each, combine them into one mega-template for "answering a negligence question."

5. Answering Problem Questions Step-by-Step

About 70% of law assessments are problem questions (you get a fictional scenario and have to "advise" someone). Here's exactly how to tackle them:

How Lecturers Write These Questions

Understanding this removes the mystery:

  • They pick 2-3 weeks of content that fit together
  • They write the "correct" answer first
  • They create a scenario where characters do things that trigger those legal issues
  • They scramble the facts so relevant info is scattered throughout the question
  • They make some issues easy (clear answer) and some hard (debatable)

Your Process (In Order)

1. Read the Whole Question Twice

First time: get the overall story. Second time: start identifying legal issues. Write them in the margin or highlight.

2. Issue Spot

For each sentence, ask: "Why is this fact here? What legal issue does it trigger?" If you have a table of contents of your templates, scan it to help you spot issues.

Write each issue as a question: "Did X commit robbery?" "Does Y owe Z a duty of care?"

3. Group the Relevant Facts Under Each Issue

Copy/paste or note which facts relate to which issue. Facts for one issue might be scattered throughout the question.

4. Plan Your Answer Order

Put the most important/obvious issues first. If one issue is a building block for another (e.g., stealing is an element of robbery), do the building block first.

5. Write: Law Then Application (For Each Issue)

Use your template to state the law. Then apply it to the facts. Here's the critical rule:

🔑 THE GOLDEN RULE OF APPLICATION:
Every sentence in your application must combine BOTH a legal principle AND the facts from the question. If you're writing about the facts without connecting them to law, it's irrelevant. If you're restating law without connecting to facts, it's just copying notes.

How to Apply Law to Facts (The Technique)

Think like a JUDGE, not a one-sided lawyer. Consider:

  1. Arguments FOR your client (the best interpretation of the facts for them)
  2. Arguments AGAINST your client (what the other side would say)
  3. Your conclusion (which argument is stronger, based on the law and cases)

Three scenarios you'll encounter:

  • Clear answer, no real debate: Apply quickly and move on. Don't waste words.
  • One side is stronger: Present both arguments, then explain why one wins.
  • Genuinely uncertain: Present both sides, acknowledge it's unclear. That's a valid conclusion.

Using Cases in Application (Arguing by Analogy)

This is what gets you from a Pass to a Distinction/HD:

  • "The facts here are similar to [Case X] where the court held [result], because [shared feature]."
  • "Unlike [Case Y] where [different fact], here [your fact] suggests a different outcome."

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ❌ Starting your answer with a summary of all the facts (never do this)
  • ❌ Only arguing one side (always consider both perspectives)
  • ❌ Writing law without applying it to facts
  • ❌ Making arguments without legal authority backing them
  • ❌ Being verbose when you could be concise (short, sharp sentences win)

6. Writing Legal Essays That Get HDs

Essays are about 20% of law assessments. They're completely different from problem questions. Instead of applying law to facts, you're critically analysing an area of law using academic research.

The Essay Process (9 Steps)

Step 1: Understand the Question

Break it down: What area of law? What's the controversy? What position is the question implying? If confused, ask AI to explain it in simple terms.

Step 2: Research (Finding Sources)

Use AI deep research + legal databases to find:

  • The relevant legislation and cases
  • 10-15 journal articles from Australian law journals
  • Government/ALRC reports if relevant

Verify every source AI finds. Download the PDFs.

Step 3: Read & Summarise Sources

For each article: attach the PDF to AI, ask it to summarise key arguments with quotes + page numbers. Then CHECK those page numbers against the PDF. Copy useful passages into your research document.

Step 4: Organise Research by Theme

Look for patterns: what arguments keep appearing? Create a new document with headings for the 3 most common arguments. Copy relevant passages under each heading.

Step 5: Develop Your Thesis

A thesis is NOT your opinion. It's an argument supported by the research. It has:

  • One central argument (what you're claiming)
  • 2-4 reasons why it's correct (your supporting arguments)

Example: "Holding children criminally responsible contradicts scientific evidence that they lack developmental maturity. NSW must raise the minimum age to be consistent with neuroscience, human rights standards, and to avoid the harm caused by early justice contact."

Step 6: Plan Your Structure

1. Introduction (thesis + roadmap, no footnotes)
2. The Law & Context (explain the current law + why it exists + counter-arguments to your thesis)
3. First Major Argument (developed with research)
4. Second Major Argument
5. Third Major Argument (if needed)
6. Conclusion (restate thesis differently, no new info, no footnotes)

Step 7: Write the Body First

Skip the intro. Write your arguments section by section. Each paragraph: topic sentence → develop argument with research → concluding sentence linking to next paragraph. Write intro and conclusion LAST.

Step 8: Proofread Multiple Times

After each sentence. After each paragraph. After each section. Print it and read aloud. Wait a day. Read again.

Step 9: AGLC Compliance Last

Only once the content is final, go through and fix all your referencing (ibid, subsequent references, pinpoints).

Writing Tips

  • Short sentences beat long ones. Always.
  • Cut every word that doesn't add meaning
  • Don't use semicolons unless you're 100% sure how
  • Avoid "it is clear that" or "it is obvious" (hyperbole weakens your argument)
  • Never have an argument without a source backing it up
  • Use headings (AGLC has specific formatting for these)

7. Statutory Interpretation

Statutory interpretation = figuring out what the words in a piece of legislation actually mean when they're ambiguous. Courts use the contextual approach (the only one that matters in practice).

The Contextual Approach (The Only One You Need)

Words must be defined in context, considering the PURPOSE of the whole Act, not just their literal meaning. Authority: CIC Insurance Ltd v Bankstown Football Club Ltd (1997) 187 CLR 384; Project Blue Sky Inc v Australian Broadcasting Authority (1998) 194 CLR 355.

The Process (Step by Step)

  1. Find the relevant section (AI can help locate it, but VERIFY on legislation.nsw.gov.au or equivalent)
  2. Quote the section in full
  3. Break it into elements (each key word/phrase that needs defining)
  4. Define each element using this hierarchy:
    • Defined in the chapter? → Use that
    • Defined in the Act's definitions section? → Use that
    • Defined in the regulations? → Use that
    • Defined in the Interpretation Act (NSW: Interpretation Act 1987)? → Use that
    • Defined by judges in case law? → Use that
    • Still ambiguous? → Apply contextual interpretation using extrinsic materials
  5. If still ambiguous, use extrinsic materials to determine the purpose:
    • Second reading speech (the Minister's speech when introducing the Bill)
    • Explanatory memoranda
    • Law Reform Commission reports
    • Objects clause of the Act (usually s 3)
  6. Interpret the word so it aligns with the Act's purpose

Extra Rules (Less Common But Good to Know)

  • Specific beats general: If two sections conflict, the specific one wins
  • Ejusdem generis (list rule): If a list of specific things is followed by "and other things," the general phrase only covers things of the same TYPE as the list
  • Noscitur a sociis: Words in a list take meaning from the other words around them
  • Criminal penalties: Sections with criminal penalties must be interpreted NARROWLY (only conduct clearly covered counts), and ambiguity favours the accused

Where to Find Extrinsic Materials

  • Commonwealth: parlinfo.aph.gov.au (Hansard) + Federal Register of Legislation
  • NSW: parliament.nsw.gov.au (Hansard) + legislation.nsw.gov.au
  • Search for: "[Bill name] second reading" in the relevant Hansard

8. Using Legal Databases Like a Pro

Legal databases are where the actual law lives. Don't use Google or your library search engine for legal research. Use these:

The Key Databases

Jade (jade.io) — Best for Finding Cases

  • Has the most Australian case law of any database
  • Citator feature: Type in a section of legislation → get every case that's considered it, ranked by relevance
  • Free with a university login
  • Download PDFs of cases here

Lexis Advance (AU) — Best All-Rounder

  • Cases, legislation, journal articles, Halsbury's Laws
  • Advanced Search → AU Cases: Enter legislation title + provision number → all cases on that section
  • Advanced Search → Secondary Materials: Find journal articles that cite a section or case
  • CaseBase: Click this under any case for a summary + "Publications referring to this case"

Halsbury's Laws of Australia (via Lexis) — Best for Summaries

  • Provides pre-written summaries of legal tests for most issues in Australian law
  • Breaks law into elements (helpful structure for your templates)
  • Paraphrase it, don't copy it (put key content in your own words + cite the original cases)

AGIS Plus Text — Best for Essay Research (Australian Journals)

  • Highest number of Australian law journal articles
  • Tick "full text only" to limit to articles you can actually read

HeinOnline — Best for International/Comparative Research

  • US and UK journal articles
  • Use when your topic crosses jurisdictions

Research Workflow for Problem Questions

  1. Identify the relevant section of legislation
  2. Use Jade Citator to find recent cases that considered that section
  3. Open the most relevant case → find where the judge summarises the law (usually after the facts section)
  4. Download the PDF → attach to AI → ask it to extract the legal test with quotes + paragraph numbers
  5. Verify the paragraph numbers against the PDF
  6. Add to your template

Research Workflow for Essays

  1. Search by case citation (e.g., "(1992) 175 CLR 1" not "Mabo") for precision
  2. Find CaseBase → "Publications referring to this case" = instant list of journal articles discussing it
  3. Search AGIS / Lexis Secondary Materials for broader topic research
  4. Use distinctive legal terms in searches (e.g., "doli incapax" not "age of criminal responsibility children")

9. Using AI Safely (Your Research Assistant, Not Your Brain)

AI is the most powerful research tool ever created. It's also the most dangerous if you use it wrong. Lawyers have literally been sanctioned for citing fake AI-generated cases in court. Here's how to use it properly:

The Golden Rules

  1. NEVER trust AI output without verification. It makes things up ("hallucinations").
  2. NEVER submit AI-generated text as your own work. That's academic misconduct.
  3. ALWAYS download the actual source and check it. Every case, every section, every article.
  4. Use AI to FIND and SUMMARISE sources, not to WRITE your answers.

The Three Methods (From Least to Most Reliable)

1. Chatbot Prompt (Least Reliable)

Just asking a question. Good for: understanding concepts, finding relevant legislation/sections, getting case names to then verify. Always verify everything it says.

2. Deep Research (Better)

Uses the research feature (ChatGPT Plus/Pro). Spends 15-20 minutes actively searching before writing a report. Less likely to hallucinate, but still needs verification.

3. Attaching Files (Most Reliable)

Download the actual PDF (case, article, legislation). Attach it to the chat. Ask questions about THAT specific document. When AI quotes from an attached file with a paragraph number, you can verify it instantly.

✅ The Verification Process:
1. AI gives you a quote and says it's at paragraph [42]
2. Open the PDF
3. Go to paragraph [42]
4. Check the quote is actually there
5. If yes → you now have verified, reliable information with a proper citation
6. If no → tell AI it's wrong and ask again, or discard it

What AI is GREAT For

  • Finding which legislation governs an issue (then you verify the section)
  • Finding cases on a specific section (then you download and verify)
  • Summarising an attached case PDF focusing on your specific issue
  • Explaining difficult legal concepts in plain English
  • Generating practice questions for spaced repetition
  • Checking your summary of law for gaps
  • Helping with AGLC citation formatting
  • Summarising attached journal articles with quotes + page numbers

What AI is TERRIBLE For

  • ❌ Writing your actual assignment (misconduct + it writes badly for law)
  • ❌ Citing cases without verification (it invents cases that don't exist)
  • ❌ Providing accurate quotes without an attached source file
  • ❌ Replacing your own thinking about how law applies to facts

The Philosophy

AI is your research assistant. You are the lawyer. The assistant finds the books, highlights the relevant pages, and organises your desk. But YOU read the material, YOU think about how it applies, YOU write the analysis, and YOU make the arguments. When exams come, the assistant can't sit with you. But because they helped you build incredible templates and practice extensively, you're ready.

10. The Spaced Practice System

Your brain forgets things unless you revisit them at increasing intervals. This is scientifically proven. Here's how to build this into your routine:

The Schedule

  • Day of tutorial: Build/update your template, answer the tutorial question
  • 1 day later: Attempt AI-generated practice question #1 WITHOUT looking at notes first. Then check against your template.
  • 1 week later: Attempt practice question #2 without notes. Check.
  • 2-3 weeks later: Attempt practice question #3 without notes. Check.
  • Exam prep: Use past exam papers as your final practice round.

How This Works in Practice

After your tutorial on (e.g.) "consideration in contract law":

  1. Tuesday (tutorial day): Build template, answer tutorial Q
  2. Wednesday: Try practice Q1 from memory, then check
  3. Next Tuesday: Try practice Q2 from memory, then check
  4. Two weeks later: Try practice Q3 from memory, then check

By the time exams arrive, you've answered 4+ questions on this topic spaced over weeks. The information is locked in.

The Retrieval Practice Principle

The act of TRYING to remember (even if you get it wrong) strengthens your memory more than re-reading your notes. So always attempt the question WITHOUT notes first, THEN check. Getting it wrong is part of the learning.

💡 Pro tip: Keep a "review calendar" in ClickUp. After each tutorial, immediately schedule the 3 follow-up practice sessions (1 day, 1 week, 2 weeks later). This way you never forget to review.

11. Assessment Countdown Protocol

4 Weeks Before Due Date

  • Read the assignment question carefully
  • Identify which weeks of content are being assessed
  • Check your templates cover those topics
  • Start any additional research needed

2 Weeks Before

  • Have a complete first draft (for assignments) OR complete set of templates (for exams)
  • For essays: all research done, thesis developed, structure planned
  • For problems: practice answering similar questions under timed conditions

1 Week Before

  • Proofread and edit your assignment, or practice past exam papers
  • Check AGLC compliance
  • Get someone to read it (study buddy, tutor, AI for proofreading only)

2 Days Before

  • Final proofread (print it, read aloud)
  • For exams: do one final practice paper under exam conditions
  • Review your templates one last time

Day Of

  • For exams: quick scan of your template table of contents, then trust your preparation
  • For assignments: submit early, don't tinker at the last second
