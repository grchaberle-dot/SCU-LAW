Topic 1: Liberal Foundations

Liberalism is the dominant ideology underpinning the Australian legal system. It emerged from the Enlightenment, replacing divine authority with reason, individual rights, and limited government.

The Social Contract

  • Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651): Life in state of nature is 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short'. Individuals surrender all freedoms to a sovereign for protection.
  • Locke (Two Treatises, 1690): State of nature not brutal. Government limited to protecting life, liberty, property. Citizens can break the contract if rulers fail.
  • Rousseau (Social Contract, 1762): State of nature pure and peaceful. Need a normative contract to protect inherent rights.

Six Core Characteristics of Liberalism

1. Liberty (positive & negative)
Negative = freedom FROM interference (the state leaves you alone)
Positive = freedom TO do things (state actively enables opportunity)
2. Individualism
The individual is the basic moral, social, legal unit. Autonomous rational beings making free choices.
3. Equality
Formal (like cases treated alike) vs Substantive (equality of outcome, addresses structural inequality)
4. Rights
Deontological (inherent/natural, Kant) vs Teleological (consequentialist, utilitarian, Bentham/Mill)
5. Rule of Law
Government constrained by law. Separation of powers. Laws predictable, non-retroactive, apply equally.
6. Toleration
Willingness to allow people to think, speak and act in ways we disapprove of.

JS Mill's Harm Principle

The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others.

On Liberty (1859). State cannot interfere with self-regarding conduct. Underpins negative liberty and classical liberalism.

Utilitarianism (Bentham & Mill)

Greatest happiness for the greatest number. Actions judged by consequences, not inherent rightness. Bentham called natural rights 'nonsense upon stilts'. Mill distinguished higher and lower pleasures.

Classical vs New Liberalism

  • Classical: Limiting government, negative liberty, free markets, laissez-faire, property rights
  • New/Modern: Positive liberty, social/economic rights, welfare state, state intervention to address inequality

Topic 2: Rule of Law & Legal Formalism

Dicey's Three Principles (1885)

  • Absolute supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power
  • Equality before the law regardless of status
  • Constitution is the consequence (not source) of individual rights as enforced by courts

Thin (Formal) vs Thick (Substantive) Rule of Law

Thin/Formal (Raz): No judgment on content of laws. Just requires: supremacy of law, equality before law, laws are general/promulgated/clear/prospective/stable. Morally neutral.
Thick/Substantive (Bingham): Rule of law MUST include protection of fundamental human rights, access to justice, fair procedures. Goes beyond procedure to moral legitimacy.

Bingham's 8 Principles

  • Law must be accessible, intelligible, clear, predictable
  • Legal questions resolved by law, not discretion
  • Law applies equally to all
  • Ministers exercise powers in good faith, fairly, within limits
  • Law must protect fundamental human rights
  • Civil disputes resolvable without prohibitive cost/delay
  • Fair adjudicatory procedures
  • State must comply with international law obligations

Rule of Law vs Rule BY Law

Rule BY law = instrumental use of law as tool of political power. State controls citizens through law but is not itself bound. E.g. China's model.

Legal Formalism

Law as self-contained system. Judges apply rules mechanically without reference to external considerations (values, social consequences). Promotes predictability and democratic legitimacy.

Critiques of Formalism:
1. Impossible (multiple laws may apply; laws over/underinclusive; judges have ideology)
2. Undesirable (morals evolve; rules have in-built deficiencies; can lead to absurd results)
3. Hides deception ('I had no choice, law constrained me')

Key Cases

Entick v Carrington (1765) - Executive can do nothing beyond what law expressly authorises. Limits of executive power.
Haneef Case (2007) - Minister's visa cancellation quashed. 'Association' requires sympathy/support/involvement in criminal conduct.
Communist Party Case (1951) - Dixon J: 'rule of law forms an assumption of the Constitution'. Federal law banning Communist Party invalid.
Al-Kateb v Godwin (2004) - Stateless person detained indefinitely. Warning about follies of literal interpretation. Migration Act silent on the issue.

Topic 3: Law & Morality / Power

Law and Morality: Connected How?

Natural Law (Non-Positivism): Law must align with superior moral imperatives. Immoral law is not law (Aquinas). Finnis: 7 basic goods (life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability, practical reasonableness, religion).
Legal Positivism: Law is law once properly enacted. No necessary connection between law and morality. Hart: law and morals should be sharply distinguished. Existence of law is one thing; its merit another (Austin).

Hart vs Fuller Debate

Hart: Minimum content of natural law needed for survival, but law/morality remain separate. Fuller: Law has 'inner morality' (8 principles of legality). A system failing these cannot claim to be law.

Consensus vs Conflict Theory (Law Emergence)

  • Functionalism: Law as expression of society's ethos, 'overly restated custom'
  • Rationalism: Laws created to rationally protect from social harm
  • Marxist Critique: Laws are instruments of dominant class. State not neutral arbitrator.
  • Moral Panics: Threats exaggerated by media/state to divert from real problems (Cohen, Marcuse)
  • Public Choice Theory: Political actors are self-interested utility maximisers, not public servants

Topic 4: Law & Equality

Formal vs Substantive Equality

Formal: Treat like cases alike. Same rules for everyone. Focus on procedure. Problem: masks pre-existing inequality, doesn't ensure genuine equality.
Substantive: Equality of outcome. Recognises some need different treatment to achieve same result. Addresses systemic/indirect discrimination. Affirmative action, special measures.

Australian Context

  • No implied right to equality in Australian Constitution: Kruger v Cth (1997)
  • Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth): s9 prohibition, s10 equality before law, s8 special measures
  • Margaret Thornton: Benchmark figure is white, Anglo-Celtic, heterosexual male

Direct vs Indirect Discrimination

Direct: Overt act treating someone differently because of protected attribute.
Indirect: Neutral rule that disproportionately excludes certain groups (systemic). Harder to prove.

Topic 5: Law & Gender

Types of Feminism

  • Liberal: Gender equality possible within existing system. Remove legal barriers. Formal equality.
  • Radical (MacKinnon): Law created by males in their image. Not about gender but dominance.
  • Cultural/Difference (Gilligan): Women have different 'voice' (ethics of care vs logic of justice). Different but not inferior.
  • Socialist: Gendered division of labour supports capitalism. Gender inequality rooted in economic structure.
  • Postmodern: Rejects grand narratives. Focuses on particular instances of oppression. Acknowledges intersectionality.

Public/Private Dichotomy

Liberal theory separates public (state intervention legitimate) from private (freedom from interference). Feminist critique: this masks domestic violence, marital rape, unpaid labour. 'Personal is political.'

Key Reforms (Australia)

  • Right to vote (1902), practice law (1918 NSW)
  • Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)
  • Marital rape outlawed NSW 1981
  • Abortion decriminalised nationally by 2019

Topic 6: Law & Race / Colonisation

Scientific Racism & Terra Nullius

Colonisation rationalised by: doctrine of terra nullius ('land belonging to no one'), obligation to cultivate, and racial hierarchy theories (Linneaus 1758).

Critical Race Theory

  • Racism is 'ordinary' and culturally constructed
  • Law not objective or value neutral
  • Formal equality masks entrenched disadvantage (Lawrence)
  • Perspective of white middle class = 'neutrality' in law
  • Moreton-Robinson: 'Patriarchal whiteness' as invisible organising principle

Frantz Fanon (Anti-Colonial Theory)

Psychology of coloniser/colonised. Language reinforces racial oppression. Colonialism (not just class) drives oppression. Independence requires de-colonial struggle.

Mabo v Queensland (No.2) (1992)

Mabo (No.2) - Rejected terra nullius as legal fiction. Recognised native title based on traditional laws/customs. BUT: left doctrine of settlement undisturbed, native title can be extinguished by government, onerous burden of proof.

Topic 7: First Nations & Law

Indigenous Law (Watson's 'Raw Law')

From the creation/Dreaming. Emphasises unity of all living things, obligations to renew land, consensus, reciprocity, relationships, justice/harmony. Contrasts with Western: land ownership, accumulation, hierarchical patriarchy, adversarial punishment.

Criminal Justice Issues

  • 3% population, 28% prison, 50% youth detention
  • Structural causes: racism, history of colonisation, crime-as-resistance, cultural difference
  • Race-neutral laws applied in discriminatory way
Walker v NSW (1994) - 'All people should stand equal before the law.' Criminal law does not accommodate alternative body of law operating alongside it. Formal equality applied.

Arguments For/Against Recognition of Indigenous Law

For: Self-determination, human rights, UNDRIP, cultural survival
Against: Legal centralism, equality before law, human rights conflicts, rule of law principles

Protection Legislation & Stolen Generations

By 1911 all states/territories had 'protection' Acts. Extensive powers to protectors: removal to reserves, control of children, prohibition of customs. REALITY: removed First Peoples from country and kin. Breached rule of law principles.

Topic 8: Human Rights

Source of Rights

  • Natural law: Inherent in being human, universal, derived from reason (Locke, Kant)
  • Positive/Conventional: Constructed by communities, enacted into law (Bentham, Hart)

Key International Instruments

  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) - aspirational
  • ICCPR (1966) - civil and political rights, binding
  • ICESCR (1966) - economic, social, cultural rights
  • CERD, CEDAW, CRC, CAT

State Obligations (Shue)

Respect (refrain from violating) | Protect (prevent third parties violating) | Fulfil (ensure full exercise of rights)

Should Australia Have a Bill of Rights?

For: Current protections inadequate, cement rights permanently, protect against majority tyranny, lagging behind other nations
Against: Responsible government sufficient, undermines parliamentary sovereignty, too much judicial power, can become outdated, 'villain's charter'

Current Protections

Partial: s51(xxxi) just compensation, s80 jury trial, s116 freedom of religion, s117 no interstate discrimination. Implied freedom of political communication (Lange). Statutory: Human Rights Act 2019 (Qld), Charter (Vic), HRA (ACT).

Topic 9: Socialism, Class & the Rule of Law

Three Socialist Perspectives

Marx & Engels: Liberal ideals are ideological justification for capitalism. True power is economic. Legal power exercised by those with economic power.
E.P. Thompson: Rule of law is 'unqualified human good' and 'cultural achievement'. Allows class struggle within a system all parties accept, rather than naked brutal warfare.
Axel Honneth: Democratic way of life = socialism's emancipated society. Enlarging public sphere creates opportunities for social change through social freedom.

Liberal Response to Marxism

Empirical: Legal reasoning has relative autonomy, not illusory.
Normative: Communist regimes (Stalin, Mao) committed worse human rights violations. Rule of law + separation of powers protects against arbitrary power.

Whitlam Government Reforms (1972-75)

Example of social change through parliamentary reform: Medibank, equal pay, free university, abolished death penalty, Family Law Act, Racial Discrimination Act 1975, Aboriginal affairs.

Topic 10: Globalisation & Counterterrorism

Globalisation & Law

  • Processes making world interdependent in economy, culture, politics
  • Globalisation OF law (domestic unification) vs BY law (international agreements change domestic law)
  • Legal pluralism: common law, civil law, Islamic law, Chinese law, indigenous law coexist
  • MNCs as drivers but also exploiters of localised conditions

Counterterrorism & the Rule of Law

Terrorism definition: serious crime + intent to spread terror + politically/religiously/ideologically motivated. s100.1 Criminal Code (Cth).

Tensions with Liberalism:
- No harm principle breached by prevention measures
- Preventive detention without crime
- Control orders with low standard of proof
- Vague laws (Thomas v Mowbray, Kirby J dissent)
- Erosion of separation of powers
- Guilt by association (bikie laws)

Dicey: 'The proper function of the state is the punishment, not the prevention, of crimes.' Counter-terrorism laws challenge this fundamental liberal principle.
